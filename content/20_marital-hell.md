title: "Marital Hell"
weight: 20

What could be more beautiful than the first stirrings of adolescent love? The body reaches its full form, the other person's genitals finally take on their full meaning, visible and invisible shapes arouse us, we think only of the bliss of a long-awaited embrace...

The wonder too often fades with the first attempts at intimacy. Sexologists blame the clumsiness of novices. Psychoanalysts denounce Oedipal traumas. Psychiatrists hardly mention it, seeing it only as a need for learning. As for lovers, they simply accept the sad reality, or sometimes wonder: why doesn't sexuality live up to its promises?

The long-term outcome of love stories, even the most beautiful ones, is often far from joyful. The magic of the honeymoon, the first romantic flights, the frenzied onslaught of new emotions, the plans for a wondrous future are most often followed by a long, slow descent, or even a crash landing. Impatience, misunderstandings, jealousy, conflict, and resentment creep into daily life. The best couples manage to conceal the inexorable deterioration from themselves, to hide it from others. Others cannot.

Poets and songwriters find an inexhaustible source of inspiration: the vast majority of poems and songs speak of love, evoking sometimes the highest bliss, sometimes painful disappointment, betrayed hope, disconcerting failure, and unhealable wounds. Singing of pain or hope might well be a way of denying a reality that contradicts our deepest expectations.

Why do so many grand passions end in tears? Artists are not logicians, yet they subtly invite us to question this fundamental contradiction: love, which our impulses lead us to place at the pinnacle of our desires, brings us more torment and heartbreak than any other existential hope. But must it inevitably be so? Is it a mistake of nature? Or is there some cause for this situation, a human error in the face of certain natural laws?

Tristan and Isolde, heroes of a "transfigurative" love, in the language of exegetes, therefore of the highest order one could hope for, also experienced the initially blissful ecstasy of love potions. Their irrepressible desire, however, would lead them to betrayal, adultery, the worst suffering, and death. How is it that such a cruel scenario has become ingrained in our history? Does such a myth reflect a resignation in the face of the injustice of reality, or rather a cry for help, a protest against a situation that we sense is not inevitable?

These questions were beginning to surface in my pre-adolescent mind. The literature I was forced to read in middle school, or that I ventured to devour under my duvet, inspired exciting wonder in me. Gide, Colette, Roger Peyrefitte, Virgil's Bucolics, certain texts by Plato, even Sophocles' Oedipus Rex (in which I played the role of the young shepherd leading Tiresias by the hand), convinced me that there are particular forms of love—not just friendship—and that this whole affair poses serious problems for humanity.

An even more cruelly prosaic and unsettling fact: the relationship between my parents. My father had told me, "You are the fruit of our love". But I didn't appreciate this lineage at all. When he kissed my mother, I was shocked by the spasms that wracked his body. He would embrace her with both arms, in close, jerky movements that gave the scene a strange, shuddering quality. As a young boy, I hadn't made the connection with any kind of pelvic rhythm, but rather with his cello-like vibrato. These encounters made me uncomfortable, disgusted me, without my knowing why.

My mother, too, apparently. Every time such an embrace took place in the kitchen, she would glance over her older partner's shoulder at sthe electric wall clock, whose second hand was undoubtedly moving too slowly. She would even complain quite regularly to my unsuspecting ears about the excessive length of these embraces and, above all, their spastic nature, which she despised and declared pathological.

She even went on a three-month strike once. After refusing, like a faithful wife, the delicate advances conveyed by a bouquet of roses offered to her by my drama teacher (whose student I was, to say the least, the object of my affections), she opened her mouth only to eat and vomit, remaining obstinately bedridden in a darkened room, to the point that my father despaired.

This strike also provided an opportunity for a truce: under normal circumstances, my mother would have a major nervous breakdown once a month, or more precisely, once a lunar cycle. I had noticed the systematic coincidence between, on the one hand, the occurrence of these periods when her gaze would gradually become heated and clouded, when she would complain of being a slave to her partner, when she would call me a monster, a sinister reincarnation of my grandfather who had always tormented her, returning to earth first as an evil sperm, clever enough to bypass the condom, for the sole purpose of making her endure the agonies of hell during an abominable childbirth... most often ending in screams that alerted the neighbors.

A systematic coincidence with a bloody discovery I also made every month in the family bidet, where a whole series of blood-stained sanitary napkins, made of absorbent gauze since no one had yet invented disposable ones, were soaking in a madder-red liquid. The sight, discovered by chance under a cloth, initially frightened me, especially since no one could explain the reasons. I had to delve into the large medical Larousse dictionary, which was kept out of my reach, for the matter to become clear. I finally understood why people spoke of a woman who was "indisposed".

I can still hear my poor father saying to me, trying to be reassuring, after she'd just alerted the upstairs neighbors with her uncontrollable screams: "Mitsou (that's the nickname he gave her) is a wonderful woman, it's just her head that's not quite right"... I thought to myself that the head was still an important organ. That it would have been better if a leg or an arm or anything else had been wrong. But, strangely enough, for the male member of the family, the most important thing was elsewhere. I provisionally conclude that love is blind. It must have been an incredibly powerful feeling to play such tricks on us...

At seven, I had tried to get my father's attention by learning the cello. For him, a career was everything. He lived only to be as famous as Pablo Casals. When the first two-track tape recorders appeared, he recorded his interpretation of Bach's suites on one track and Casals's on the other. He would play the two versions alternately for each visitor, to prove that he made far fewer mistakes and that only the injustice of the world had prevented him from becoming so famous. For years, I followed in his footsteps, living only for my glory as a future soloist. It took cancer to make me think.

I can still hear my mother, as if it were yesterday, speaking to me in a professorial tone about three subjects she considered essential: syphilis, reincarnation, and Freemasons. The three secrets one had to know to be invulnerable and successful in life.

First subject: syphilis. It was recognizable in the children of syphilitic couples; they had an abnormally flat back to their skull. Hence the need for a sharp moral compass, for absolute respect for the vows of fidelity that underpin marital happiness and the spiritual fulfillment of existence. Indeed, it was enough to make love once with an infected person to contract the diabolical spirochete, which spiraled up from the genitals all the way to the spinal cord, to ravage the brains of its victims and make them pay the price of their sin with madness. Even Nietzsche had caught it with a prostitute, those horrible women who were paid to lend their vaginas to male lust. He deserved it.

My mother regularly complained to me about the way my father made love, or rather, made her endure it. She described the back-and-forth excitement she impatiently awaited the end of, then the weight that crushed her, and the disgust that suffocated her. During one of these confidences, I swore to her that, once I grew up, if I too were to fall in love, I would remain perfectly still. As if I had sensed that another kind of amorous magic might govern adult lovemaking.

I had noticed something odd: every two weeks, my parents treated themselves to a movie, which was no small expense for the family budget. I would ask them why it was so important to them, and why they didn't take me, since I didn't like being alone in that large apartment (at least not before I discovered it was an opportunity to bring my closest friends over, or to listen to banned radio plays).
My mother would always reply, with a very heavy tone, that she needed to "clear her head". The phrase stuck in my mind. One day, I cheekily pointed out to her that this must be proof that her ideas were flawed. This earned me a memorable beating, the kind I regularly experienced. I can still see the tiled floor of the entryway, dedicated to these sacrificial ceremonies, on which I would roll around screaming, a checkerboard of white and brownish-purple squares, just inches from my eyes. The image still echoes with my mother's shrill invectives, calling me a monster.

This didn't explain the apparent urgency of those movie nights. But little by little, still wide awake after my escapades or my radio plays, which I only interrupted when I heard the elevator stop on the floor, I noticed another significant pattern: the large slatted blind in my father's room, which opened onto a large balcony, and onto which also opened a small French window from my mother's room, where I occupied one of the twin beds since her snoring had become unbearable for her partner's eardrums... The blind, therefore, would noisily come down about fifteen minutes after we returned from the cinema. It was impossible for me to go and see what was happening in my father's room. Then there was silence for about half an hour. I didn't understand why my mother hadn't come to bed. And after half an hour, the sounds of a toilet and a shower echoed from the other end of the apartment.

It was the next morning that the mystery was solved: as I went to dry myself after washing my face, I took a towel from the drying rack at the foot of the bathtub, and there, carefully hidden, I found a surprising transparent rubber object, drying, no doubt for some future use. I finally recognized it by its shape and size as the infamous condom my mother had mentioned, though I hadn't quite understood what she was saying. Everything became clear: the sperm that had somehow managed to sneak in, the movie as a prelude to a more lively evening—too lively, since my mother was complaining about her husband's intense pelvic activity...

The second major topic: reincarnation. The theory was of utmost importance, no doubt to justify the idea that I was the evil avatar of my grandfather. It also served to make me obey, because any disobedience would be punished, even if I escaped it in this life, during a haunting return to earth. My mother was undoubtedly sincere. She herself needed to believe in a form of damnation to explain why she had been the martyred child of her family (the second youngest of nine children in nine years), and why the happiness she expected in her marriage had turned into an ordeal and enslavement, to use the words she would chant on special occasions.

Having dubbed herself a "psychographer", enamored of Hinduism and Tibetan wisdom, and regularly reading tarot cards, she ultimately instilled in me a visceral distrust of anything that might evoke esotericism or pseudoscience. One of my memories concerns a first cousin who came to spend the holidays, whose handwriting she examined. Without mincing words, she delivered her verdict: at thirteen, he already had the handwriting of a murderer. The poor man confessed to me at seventy that it had scarred him his entire life.

And the third subject: the Freemasons. They were everywhere, watching for the slightest opportunity to destroy careers. They were the ones who had forbidden the audience from applauding at a concert by the Lausanne Chamber Orchestra, of which my father was the principal cellist, after he had performed Haydn's famous concerto. He was very proud of having overcome the technical difficulties of this landmark work for the instrument. No doubt his interpretation lacked the flair of a great soloist. But there was no doubt: the failure had been Machiavellianly orchestrated by these shadowy figures, who recognized each other by a certain way of shaking hands, and who worked to destroy anything that didn't conform to the sect's tenets.

It took me years to rid myself of these beliefs. At the time, at the tender age of eleven, I actually set about converting all my classmates. Some followed, but the majority cried madness. They called my followers the "Burgerians". When I persisted, they even decided to stone me on my way home from school. I complained to my Latin teacher, my class leader. I can still see him explaining, with a contrite air, that it wasn't his responsibility and that I had to learn to fend for myself. Back then, harassment and hazing were strangely tolerated.

The feeling of abandonment and injustice that rose within me only intensified each time I encountered my tormentors. Hidden in a cave or behind a bush in the park I had to cross to get back to the family home, they would attack me by surprise with stones that frightened me more than they hurt me. But a panic-stricken fear would make me turn back until I was breathless, or I would flee forward, my heart pounding, to escape them. This did little to instill in me a sense of a protective and benevolent society. It did, however, help me to break free from my mother's teachings, and later, by extension, from certain stereotypes of social thought.

Another crucial topic I almost forgot: masturbation. My mother had hinted that some depraved children "touched themselves" (she hadn't specified what), and that through these unclean gestures, they were seeking a "sensation". I didn't dare tell her that I'd discovered the secret before the age of ten, quite by chance while reading Jules Verne's *The Carpathian Castle*, whose length had led me to seek a more hedonistic companion. My first orgasm was more like an electric shock. Contrary to all expectations, I felt the urge to repeat the experience three days later.

My mother's advice was undoubtedly intended to be preventative. Like when she gave me the famous "*De onania*" by the renowned Dr. Tissot, published in 1750, a copy of which she had carefully preserved. I thus learned that masturbation not only caused deafness, but that it was the cause of a whole host of illnesses, ranging from pallor (unfortunately, I was very pale at the time) to cancer, including asthenia and all sorts of ailments I hadn't the slightest idea about.

My poor mother must have felt that the lesson wasn't sinking in. She sought out increasingly blunt arguments. The most striking was that of the Spartans: these ascetic Greeks threw children who masturbated off cliffs to their deaths in the sea. This didn't stop me, even though I resolved each time to sin no more, and ended up succumbing to temptation as it became more irresistible.

Something told me it was a natural process: indeed, no need for artifice, condoms, abortion, or theatrics to reach orgasm. Why was it so harmful? I found the long culinary preparations to which my mother, who fancied herself a gourmet cook, devoted entire mornings or afternoons, covered in sweat and cursing her enslavement in front of the gas stove that reeked of grease, far more artificial.

The longest day was the one my father discovered, first thing in the morning, in the act, sitting on the edge of the bed. He was so breathless he couldn't utter a single syllable, and left, his eyes downcast, nodding his head helplessly. It was my mother who lectured me again. After I got home from school, she implied that I would never find that famous "sensation" in my future married life. I'm now convinced she never experienced orgasm. She also pointed out that you could recognize homosexuals just by looking at them. Their eyebrows were abnormally close together, a bit like Cyclopes'. And of course, they had become homosexual because they had masturbated.

Summarized like that, the picture of my childhood seems rather disastrous. But in the end, it was actually a blessing in disguise. The harsh reality of daily life, my mother's neurosis, my father's paranoia, the quest for glory, and the obsession with persecution left me with no illusions about marital bliss, the comfort of the family cocoon, the grand myth of the eternal couple, or even social success. The context was too ludicrous, too bitter, too painful for me to project my identity onto it. Without losing a certain gratitude for my parents, who paradoxically seemed to be doing their best, I rebuilt my own freedom, my personality far removed from convention, and my own world of love in a different sphere. A more comfortable childhood would have settled me into the prevailing certainties and stereotypes, whereas the underlying suffering pushed me to seek something else, to worry about the causes of the conflicts and contradictions within the system. To seek my happiness in a more magical world, one that I sensed existed.

Ultimately, it was a blessing in disguise, because it led me to rediscover an approach to love and sexuality free from the social and moral constraints that perpetuate its false image from generation to generation. The hell of family life, beneath its cozy exterior, was intolerable enough to convince me not to settle into it myself.

I certainly made a great effort to conform later on by getting married and having children, but my mind was already attuned to all the pitfalls of this form of happiness. It took me about ten years of marriage to recognize, in agreement with my wife, its singular absurdity. In fact, I sensed all of this from a very young age.

On my bedside table sat a small blond angel made of white-painted plywood, holding a small candle in its hands. I would gaze at it at night, for long periods, when I was allowed to light the wick, as if it were a promise of a better world, a more ethereal existence, like when you look at the stars, or breathe in the sea breeze. The flickering flame evoked in me the fragility of an unknown light, in which no one could forbid me to hope.

I also had a small flashlight, which I turned on under my covers so as not to attract attention through the glass doors. I watched the projection of the cone of light onto the sheets, oval, round, parabolic depending on the distance and angle. I also made shadow puppets with it. These ever-changing patches of light gave me the impression of escaping into another world. Sometimes I shone a phosphorescent brooch on a small white dog with two large eyes, whose textured fur retained the light long after it was turned off, as if it had been nourished by some mysterious energy.

I felt the same sense of mystery watching my electric train go by, with the lights I had installed in the cars. The hum of the locomotive, the remote power of the electricity I controlled from my transformer, the shifting shadows cast on the walls of the room, plunged me into a kind of state of grace. Time stood still, giving way to a feeling of eternity, to an ineffable happiness... Seemingly insignificant details, which symbolically answered a deep yearning, a need to find somewhere an enchantment, a world of transcendence that was missing from everyday life.

But where could this other universe be hidden?
