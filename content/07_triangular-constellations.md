In its time, although condemned by the Church, this was more common than one might think, at least in some circles. Leonardo da Vinci, Michelangelo, Erasmus, for example, alleguedly got into a lot of troubles of this kind with the clergy. The Renaissance was the cradle of neo-Platonicism, and this return to Ancient Greece probably did not go without questioning morality.

It must be borne in mind that Plato, contrary to the interpretations of certain pudibund exegetes who instituted the concept of "platonic love" as excluding any physical relationship, did not condemn sexual realization: he clearly speaks of *giving in* to the desire of the erast. Sodomy is certainly stigmatized in *Laws*, a last-hour work which, if it is by the hand of the great philosopher, caricatures the future of a society without celestial Eros: it would be risky to draw moralizing conclusions from it. In Symposium, Socrates refuses Alcibiade's advances, but the text states that this rejection, outrageous for the young disciple, is motivated by the deal he meant to make, trading his beauty in exchange for the Master's wisdom: such calculation flies in the face of the celestial Eros.

Note that sodomy was common in many other cultures. For example, in Japan, she did not shock anyone until Westerners forced their entry in 1868. Five years later, the government banned it, for fear of Japanese be classified as barbaric peoples. So we ought rather be inspired by a certain cultural relativism rather than an ethnocentric interpretations of this disconcerting scene.

Much closer to us, a striking example of the possible links between sodomy and transcendent inspiration is given to us by the so-called short story of a couple of poets: Verlaine and Rimbaud. During the brief period of harmony they experienced, their creativity reached their peak. No poet ever equal such productivity or capacity for inspiration and renewal as the youngest of the two manfested. Then the jealousy of Verlaine's wife compelled Verlaine to put an end to the forbidden idyll. It did not take him long to regret it, but Rimbaud in turn, hurt by rejection, denied him. Verlaine, drunk as usual and in intense suffering, took a revolver and shot the young rebel. A trial then revealed their kind of relationship, unambiguously reflected in this famous answer from the accused to the judge who asked him if he was really a "sodomist": "One says *sodomite*, sir".
Rimbaud's inspiration completely vanished as a result of the breakup, to the point that he was exiled to the colonies and became a gun dealer, without writing a single more verse, ending in pitifully dying of the amputation of a gangrenous leg. This unfortunate adventure brought to the world a flood of poems that transformed French poetry, and above all, more silently, an awareness of the relationship between sexuality and creativity. The excellent film "Rimbaud Verlaine" with Leonardo di Caprio and David Thewlis passed on this essential message to this day.
