It would be abusive to decipher Bosch's message by reducing it an exclusive disjunction now universally accepted between heterosexuality and homosexuality. The first thinker believed to have forged this dichotomy, Karl-Maria Kertbeny, actually *stigmatized* unisexuality ( being either only hetero or homosexual), to which he opposed the "normalsexualität". He was known in his time to fight anti-Sodomite morality. He argued that both homosexual and heterosexual relationships are innate and permanent tendencies, and that the law has no business stigmatizing private sexual acts between consenting adults. Generally forgotten today, he was in fact the forerunner of the liberation of homosexuality, which took place in the United States in 1974, a century later. His grave, recently rediscovered in Budapest, is honoured every year by some gay movements.

Denominations inaugurated by Kertbeny were taken up by the infamous Baron Richard von Krafft-Ebing, author of the founding work of psychiatry, *while inverting completely their meaning*: "De psychopathia sexualis" (1912). For him, everything but "normal coitus" was pathological. Even today, we stay deeply marked by this "normative" moral. Freud himself couldn't get away completely. He postulated the existence of a fundamental bisexuality, specific to child structures, and considered homosexuality as the result of a cessation of normal sexual development.

In Bosch's day, the anathema of sin, established by the Holy Catholic Church, used to apply to both extramarital coitus and sodomy. However, we must rethink our image of the morality of the time: it was characterized by immanent duplicity. On one hand, there were the demands of official morality, the conduct and the discourses publicly displayed, on the other, what people lived in the intimacy of everyday life and thoughts one kept to himselfs. Minds were not yet molded on the rationalistic model to the point of necessarily applying laws outside privacy. One only sought to avoid the mortal intrusions of the Inquisition.

Another author can enlighten us on this point. In a book entitled "L'invention de la culture heterosexuale", Louis Georges Tin, professor of literature at the University of Lyon, demonstrates that it is from courtly love that a desire appeared in certain circles to transpose the values of homosexuality into heterosexual relations. The Church first opposed this movement, for about five centuries, and finally consecrated it by creating the sacrament of marriage. Previously, marriage was more a matter of blood or money, than a sacralization of love. It is not easy to correct the prism through which we decipher our own history.

However, this is the sine qua non condition for a proper reading of Bosch's message.
