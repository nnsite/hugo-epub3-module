I could think of nothing but him, Sabu, the young Indian actor who played Mowgli in The Jungle Book. The first great film in Technicolor, made while war raged, those scenes of virgin forest, of magnificent nature, those wild animals, those treasures guarded by the cobra, the innocence and skill of the child raised by wolves, had moved me deeply. But this moving experience was not of the same order as the emotions I had felt until then.

I was between nine and ten years old and, for the first time, I was in love. I pleaded with my parents with me to send him a letter. They couldn't dissuade me. Writing at that age was difficult, so I poured all my writing skills and calligraphic ingenuity into it, using green ink, my favorite, as if it gave me more hope. My father could only calm me by accompanying me to the cinema to give the duly stamped envelope to the cashier, who smiled and promised to forward it.

For weeks, for months, I drew tarot cards every day, hoping they would announce his arrival. In the evenings, before falling asleep, I made a large space for him in my bed, settling myself close to the edge, certain that one day I would find him there in the morning. For lack of anything better, I clutched a small stuffed monkey to my heart, which I planned to give him as a welcome gift. I still remember that time as the first truly happy period of my childhood. My parents and my teacher tried everything to bring me back to reality. My mind was elsewhere, my heart soaring too high for anyone to touch it. The days passed differently, as if carried away by clouds of light.

But time went by, and still no answers. My tarot readings were too often negative. The friends I told about Sabu remained distant. My parents became increasingly indifferent, irritated. I even ended up abandoning my little monkey. Life was becoming gray again. Starting middle school and the fear of hazing had erased the wonder. I retained only a tiny seed of it in a corner of my heart.

Then one day, as my father was walking me out of the Conservatory after my cello lesson, we met an eminent professor. I can still see him, twice my height, wearing a beige gabardine coat with large amber buttons. His face, his almond-shaped eyes, his quiet presence, I felt as if I were looking at a Buddha's smile, like the one on the statuette my parents displayed on the living room mirrored console, as a tribute to their Hindu leanings.

Intimidated, I asked my father what he taught. The professor himself answered, in a voice that deeply moved me with its delicacy and depth: "Dramatic art, and also diction". When I remained silent, he added, with a kind smile: "I train theater actors". I ventured awkwardly: "And what is diction?" "The art of pronouncing words well, of linking them smoothly, to be understood clearly, whether on a theater stage or speaking in public"...

My father watched this exchange with a slightly bewildered expression, silent. As if he sensed that something else was happening. I asked my interlocutor again what his name was: "Paul Pasquier", he said, glancing at me slightly out of the corner of his eye. Then I turned to my father, on whom everything would depend: "Can I take diction lessons?" “He didn’t dare refuse them; it would have looked bad.

I felt a kind of victory. Not just for having found an escape from family life, but for a much deeper reason, one I couldn’t quite grasp. All the love I had felt for Sabu was magically transferred to this man of the theater. Perhaps because of his artistic sensibility. I had a vague feeling that he, too, had felt something when he spoke to me.

The diction class, which was given in a group, wasn’t for another week. I thought of nothing else, along with the worry of being faced with students much older than me. I can still see the large hall of the conservatory, with its rows of seats, white-painted wood upholstered in red velvet. He was in the third row. In front of him, a whole line of students next to whom I had to sit.” They were all grown-ups, very self-assured, and rather disdainful of the newly arrived junior.

I was the last to go up on the podium. We had to repeat quickly and loudly an exercise that seemed quite trivial to me: matama matamat ma, me te me me te met me, mitimi... with every possible vowel and diphthong. So I gave it a go in my little child's voice, and I was applauded for a first flawless performance.

As the weeks went by, there was a whole range of increasingly difficult exercises, in which I strove to shine, not to outdo my competitors or to please my teacher, but because my heart led me to do it and I felt no effort at all. At the end of the semester, a competition was held: who could say the trickiest little phrase, "I want and I demand", the most times in one breath and without a single mistake? (Try it: very few succeed on the first try...). I practiced all week and managed to repeat it thirty-five times without pausing for breath or making a single slip of the tongue. I had won first prize...

As a result, Mr. Pasquier entered me in another competition. They needed to find a child to recite a long poem in a play celebrating the impending end of the war: "Battements du monde" (The Heartbeat of the World), by William Aguet. It was May 1944 at Radio-Lausanne; I wasn't even ten years old. Alone in front of my microphone, in a gigantic hall, with a whole stage production around me and a choir of about a hundred singers behind me, I only had to think of my teacher to forget even the slightest stage fright. The long prayer, though I didn't fully grasp its significance, encapsulated the long journey I would later undertake to try to understand the world's suffering.

Then, after a series of small radio drama roles, during which I discovered the hidden mechanics, the secrets of production, and the actors' bawdy humor, came a child's role in a real play: "Le déjeuner d'amoureux" (The Lovers' Luncheon) by Jean Giraudoux. A divorced husband, on a romantic rendezvous with his mistress, is surprised by the arrival of his son in the middle of his meal. He had forgotten it was visiting day and, unable to send him away, dismisses the beautiful woman, who storms off in a rage, leaving the father to rediscover with his son the love he had never been able to give him.

A tour was organized throughout French-speaking Switzerland, featuring this play. It took numerous rehearsals and many exercises to convince me to speak loudly enough on stage. Mr. Pasquier invited me many weekends to rehearse in his apartment, which seemed to me like a magician's lair. Every detail appeared to me as a world unto itself, a source of wonder. I dreamt only of the moments when he would take me on his lap to mime the final scenes. Or perhaps it was because he felt the same emotion as I did... Soon came the public performances. The smell of makeup, all those people entering the theater, the three fateful knocks, the stage fright that rose as my entrance approached...

Everything transformed into happiness the moment I saw my adoptive father, unflappable under the stage lights, elicit laughter and sighs from the audience, sharply dismissing that teacher who understood nothing of our love. Then, I would end up blissfully nestled on his lap, bathed in his tenderness, my feelings carried by the tears of the female spectators...

What would Giraudoux have thought? As the performances continued, a wonderful, mutual love blossomed between us. He invited me for a month's holiday in one of those charming little Valais valleys, where he and his older sister had rented a chalet. My parents seemed relieved, as if a burden had been lifted. They asked no questions, or at least, they didn't ask me any. And so I was able to spend four weeks in near-perfect bliss...

The place was idyllic: the fragrant mountain grasses, the jagged rocks I climbed and jumped over, pretending to be a mountain goat, the view over the valley and the high, snow-capped peaks, the bright sunshine, the pure air, the log cabin covered with large, flat stones—a true fairy tale. Only Mr. Pasquier's sister, rather stiff and cross-eyed to boot, seemed to play the role of the wicked witch. No doubt she sensed this delicate connection between her brother and me and assumed the role of chaperone.

It's true that I dreamed of sleeping in his bed, of being even closer to him than on stage. When you feel so good next to someone, on their lap, bathed in their warmth, why deny yourself closer contact? The pull was so strong that I did everything I could to achieve my goal. I even decided to abstain from masturbation altogether, as if out of respect for something sacred. But the wicked fairy, in front of her silent brother, gave me a lecture with a strange smile: "Sleeping with Paul... No! Those are things you just don't do, period".

The return home was all the more brutal. As if something had broken during the holidays. I felt very happy, having spent all that time with the one I loved; it was even unexpected. But with a caveat, a hint of failure. A bit like Sabu's refusal to join me, even though I made plenty of room for him by my side every night.

It's difficult to recount all these intimate upheavals. Even if they were presented in a conventional way, like the plot of a classic novel. There, authors can give free rein to their imagination without risk. Not only are these stories in which they are not personally involved (or they don't say so), but above all, their experiences fall within the broad paradigms of normality. I wouldn't undertake the delicate task of exposing the indecent truths of my own experience here, were it not for the necessity of explaining how I came to discover a completely unknown connection, at least in the West, between love and certain faculties that are also poorly understood. A destabilizing subject whose importance, or even existence, I would never have suspected, had it not been led to it by experience. The account of my own experience should facilitate its understanding.

My return from vacation coincided with my entry into eighth grade (the third year of middle school). Hence the arrival in the class of a number of new students. There was one who was two years behind, therefore having failed two years in a row. He initially aroused contempt in me. I was consistently top of my class and didn't think much of myself. How could I possibly fail two promotions out of three grades?

He was much taller than average, but rather shy and reserved. Blue eyes, a little pale. His voice had already broken. In just a few days, my initial disdain completely reversed. I surprised myself. A few small conversations during recess had awakened in me a feeling even more powerful than my previous romances. I even went so far as to ask the homeroom teacher if he could sit next to me. Luckily, I was alone on my bench, perhaps to prevent anyone from copying my tests. The homeroom teacher agreed after a brief negotiation.

Things were more complicated with the German teacher. A tall, thin, authoritarian man who found my request ridiculous. I was fortunate enough to have my seat to myself. What a difference it made whether you sat there or somewhere else. But I insisted so much that he finally gave in, not without sending me a sarcastic remark that still rings in my ears: "If that's enough for your happiness"... It was the same teacher who, three years later, became terribly jealous when I wanted to give Latin lessons to two twins whom he had taken under his wing...

This reversal of disdain into passion began when the teacher asked the new students to introduce themselves, to describe their tastes and favorite pastimes. François was passionate about photography (his older sister was taking professional courses) and also about electronics. I was impressed by his knowledge. But it was something else, something deeper, that stirred this irresistible impulse within me. I felt him as a young god. As if there were something sacred in him, or between him and me. Like when you're in a cathedral and the sun's rays make the stained-glass windows sparkle. The feeling that, beyond appearances, something surreal is hidden, the promise of a soaring of the soul above the peaks and clouds...

Clearly, he too fell in love with me. He invited me every weekend during the school year, and even during the holidays. I lived the rest of the time for those moments of happiness. I hopped on my bicycle and pedaled effortlessly up the steepest streets of Lausanne. He lived on the other side of town. It felt like we were right next door.

His mother, from a very good Vaudois family, seemed fascinated by the charm of our relationship. His sister, in her early twenties, already dumped by a previous fiancé, mocked us with a cheeky grin. I thought her voice sounded like a watering can. As for his father, a tall, broad Swiss-German man with round glasses and a bit of a belly, he initially paid us no attention, preoccupied with his work as a salesman.

It was when we were alone in his room that the important moments happened. I can still see, against my cheek, the fine knit of his cardigan, green striped with gray. When I snuggled up to him, my head reached just above his heart. Hearing it beat was exhilarating. He held me tenderly in his arms, and we remained silent. I felt as if an invisible energy flowed between us. These chaste embraces filled me with ineffable joy. All the family troubles, the maternal crises, were nothing anymore. It was as if I had been transported to another dimension.

His family owned an old house on the shores of Lake Murten. He invited me to spend a whole weekend there. His father had a DKW, his sister sat in the front, and we—his mother, him, and I—were on the back seat. Every turn was an opportunity to snuggle closer to him. He had put his arm around my shoulders. His mother couldn't help but say, "How cute they look, François with his little friend in his arms"... His sister cast a brief, disdainful glance back. The father paused for a long silence, the weight of which I didn't immediately grasp. We felt as if we were in a celestial realm; the world only half existed.

When we arrived at the family estate, he showed me around the large, stately house. Beautiful facades, sculpted windows. A gigantic kitchen, paved with red bricks, with a large hearth and an immense table where the servants used to eat. Long corridors with numerous bedrooms, only a small fraction of which were still in use. He also showed me the clock, with its fascinating, centuries-old mechanisms. Then he showed me the twin bedroom that had been assigned to us.

He also showed me around the grounds, shaded by tall trees with magnificent foliage. The edge of the lake, with a sailboat, "La Merlette"—I haven't forgotten its name. A charming picnic spot, surrounded by thuja trees, where an old round stone table, adorned with moss, stood—perfect for a secluded picnic. I felt like I was in a fairy tale again, but this time, it was a magical palace that Aladdin had conjured from his lantern...

Night soon fell. The question didn't even arise. It seemed obvious that we should sleep in the same bed. Pressed close together, bathed in a strange vibration that exhilarated us and made us forget everything. We spoke softly, passionately, about all sorts of things. Our hands, rather chaste, ignited the touch of our bodies. I felt both transported and protected, not so much by him as by the magic emanating from our closeness. It was without a doubt the most beautiful night of my young life. It left me with the feeling of a pure, wonderful, mysterious love—I would say transcendent today—that nothing could ever tarnish. An unusual trust in life, in others, in the future.

Our only regret was that there wouldn't be another weekend before the summer holidays. His father had a very busy schedule, always on the road. I must say, I preferred him away. François did too, for that matter. One afternoon, he burst into the room and caught us very close, which clearly upset him. He'd called out to us, "Couldn't you go play some football, or do something other than stay inside in this weather!"

Neither François nor I liked football, or sports in general. I'd rowed before I met him, in a club on Lake Geneva. I enjoyed rowing in a yawl, but the unsportsmanlike, falsely macho atmosphere in the locker room quickly made me give it up. Besides, there were rumors that the instructor had questionable behavior with his students. He regularly showered with a young chaperone. It was so far removed from our own experience. These forms of camaraderie seemed hollow, devoid of substance.

Demonstrations of strength, of superiority, the very spirit of competition struck us as abhorrent. All our school friends lived with the idea of being stronger, bolder, if not crueler, than everyone else. The older children bullied the younger ones, poking them with spikes on the stairs, tripping them, and playing ball with their schoolbags. The younger children were forced to toughen up to cope with the surrounding sadism. We had to be very discreet if we didn't want our special friendship to become the laughingstock of the school, or even the pretext for more violent abuse.

Despite everything, we had excellent results throughout the school year. I was top of my class by a wide margin in all three terms, and François was close behind. We drew from our love a kind of ease in learning and understanding that took away any feeling of effort. Every difficult week was swept away by the exhilaration of the weekend. I don't think there was a single weekend we didn't spend together.

Then came the summer holidays. 1947 was a particularly hot and dry summer. I spent the first month alone with my parents in their chalet in the Pre-Alps and an uncle who was a mechanic. He had come to repair an engine for the cable car that carried luggage up from the small train station. I was interested to see up close how an internal combustion engine works. But I was counting down the days.

 Finally, the day arrived for my departure to Lake Murten. François's father had limited my stay to two weeks, supposedly for cost reasons. His motivation seemed clearer that very first evening when he burst into the room, found us both entwined in the same bed, and exclaimed, with a perfect Swiss-German accent: "Z'est bas bossible, on tireait tes samoureux!" I can still see him slamming the door. He must have talked to his wife about it, considered a proper separation, but luckily, he ended up backing down.

Mothers are generally more sensitive than fathers to their children's emotional experiences. This is undoubtedly due to the greater subtlety of female sexuality. The father positions himself as a rival, fueled by a certain jealousy, while the mother identifies more closely with the child, whose impenetrable emotions she senses. Even today, I am amazed and grateful for the idea of this mother who shared her son's unconventional love for another boy. It was as if she valued the intrinsic worth of this love more than any social conventions it might transgress.

We ourselves cared nothing for any possible rebukes. Our hearts beat too strongly and too deeply when we were close to imagine that anyone could destroy the magic that held us together. No trace of guilt; everything was pure wonder, from the sailboat rides to the car test drives in the park, to the ping-pong matches (I hadn't even considered that this is where my love of table tennis comes from). We also amused ourselves by inventing all sorts of puns. Our only worry was knowing that these days of complete happiness were numbered.

And what about sex in all this? I had once again forbidden myself any masturbation, out of respect for what was, or what could have been. François confessed to me, much later, that he too had had more specific urges and regretted having forbidden them. The prohibitions of upbringing, the asexual image that society tries to project onto children, prevented us from going much further than light touching, under the pretext of comparing sizes. It's a common concern for all teenagers to observe their rapid, even shocking, physical changes.

What we were searching for, without fully realizing it, was far beyond measurements of diameter or length: it was the extraordinary synergy of connection that occurs when hearts are in sync. When souls vibrate on the same wavelength and a profound love flows between people. Body and mind become one; it's no longer a question of localized pleasure as in a "disconnected" sexuality. Our entire bodies were Eros. Everything organ-like became secondary.

Thus, with a blissful acceptance, I was able to come to terms with the fact that I would soon be transformed. The adolescent form shouldn't necessarily destroy the subtlety of emotions, as it seemed to have in the other adults who tried so hard to play the macho role, or who had simply hardened. This was one of the things that disgusted me: the way they talked about girls: "Your girl, my chick, you're still a virgin"... or courted them while thinking only of sex. As if these were insults to the dignity of the girl, and even more so to the sacred dimension of love.

Fifteen days had been too short. François managed to coax his father's permission to accompany me on the return journey and to spend the last few days of vacation in our pre-Alpine chalet. It was like a gift from heaven. I didn't know he was poisoned. We lived those unexpected days in a shared ecstasy. The superb view of the vast Lake Geneva, the colorful mushrooms that stirred the moss in the forests, the countless flowers in those natural meadows, the streams carving little canyons in the rocks, the showers under the waterfalls, the high-altitude sun, the starry skies of nights still unpolluted...

September 4th, my thirteenth birthday, was approaching. François asked me what I would like. Overjoyed, I didn't need anything. I told him that the best gift would be the one he chose for me. We had to return to Lausanne before classes resumed to buy school supplies. It was a strange feeling, a void, almost a sense of withdrawal, to find myself alone in my bed.
