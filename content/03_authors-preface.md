Hieronymus Bosch has already been the victims of hundreds of analysts. They said everything and its opposite about his art and his inner demons, alas, nothing truly conclusive. The "devil-maker" remains a devil-maker, unanimously declared a genius, but nothing more. Having failed to understand him, even the most learned have come to believe that his triptychs simply have no meaning. The introduction by Claude Mettra, distinguished commentator for France Culture, to a 1977 book devoted to The *Garden of Earthly Delights*, is the best touchstone: "What is presented to us here is an absolute masterpiece since no rational reading, no symbolic interpretation, no historical reference can account for it. (...) It is in this sense that it is necessary to say first that Hieronymus Bosch's painting means nothing, no more than a face, no more than a field of snow under the red winter sun, no more than a piece of withered bark in the deserted forest"....

Another revealing comment, serving as a conclusion to a 2001 work, "The ABCs of Hieronymus Bosch", by Roger-Henri Marijnissen, Doctor of Art History, and Peter Ruyfelaere, historian and director of Ludion publishers, states: "Much historical research, for example with the collaboration of specialists in botany and ornithology, is still needed to arrive at a correct interpretation of Bosch's symbolism". Even more recent is this conclusion by Hans Belting, a German anthropologist and epistemologist at the forefront of medieval historiography and art criticism, who abandons the hope of deciphering any message in Bosch's work: "The ambivalence of pictorial language here surpasses the enigma of content and opens up for painting this new space of freedom where it becomes an art on the same level as poetry". (2005). There's nothing new either in José Luis Lopez-Linares's recent film, "The Mystery of Hieronymus Bosch", historically interesting but devoid of explanations. The only exception, singularly overlooked by most critics, is Wilhelm Fraenger, a Berlin professor of art history and a leading expert on the art of painting, who, between the two world wars, stumbled upon the minutes of an Inquisition trial. In the statements of the members condemned to be burned at the stake in 1411, he rediscovered the secret teachings of the "Free Spirit" sect. These teachings, concerning an "Adamic" love predating the disgrace of the Fall, were supposedly bequeathed to us through Hieronymus Bosch in the form of three cryptographic triptychs. Fraenger's thesis, recorded under the title "The Millennial Kingdom of Hieronymus Bosch" (1947), despite its erudition and penetrating power, remained virtually a dead letter. Perhaps because it caused too much embarrassment, or because it contained too many contradictions. In particular, no historical evidence suggests that Bosch had any contact with the Free Spirit.

What I propose here is a completely different approach, more modest but more certain, because it is based on concrete experience: the experience, perhaps due to chance, of a form of love different from the norm, the exact illustration of which I found in this "Garden of Earthly Delights". A form of Eros with a non-procreative end, which culminates in what the owl at the center of Paradise tells us through its large golden eyes, and which is perfectly illustrated in the profusion of the central panel. Whose failure is paid for by a thousand frustrations and sufferings, if we are to believe the merciless visual inventory in Hell.

When the key to a coded message is discovered, the rule is that each word finds its meaning. Everything simplifies, becomes clear as if by magic. The profusion in which attempts at deciphering were entangled gives way to clear and monosemic guidelines. The very complexity that confounded understanding is transformed into a recurrence of evidence. It is this observation, seeing all the hermetic motifs of this work, among the most intricate in the history of painting, fall into a simple logic, that convinces me I have found the key to the mystery.

Not that I claim to have discovered the definitive and exhaustive meaning of the triptych of triptychs. A feeling of clarity, of coherence, nevertheless compels me to share what I feel to be the solution to the enigma. Starting with submitting my thesis to the scrutiny of those who have already examined these innocent nudes, these giant fruits, and these impenetrable monsters. Some will contribute to my analytical effort, while others will demolish it, as is customary in the world of criticism.

My approach will undoubtedly surprise some. I myself am torn: both reassured by the clarity and convergence of meanings, and uneasy about bearing such a burden alone. For the ultra-reductionist rationalist I was at the dawn of adulthood, it was a harsh lesson. An entire edifice of positivist convictions collapsed in the face of the evidence of an experience that remained inexplicable within the conventional framework. And today, the pinnacle: the spiritualist approach of the Berlin specialist is still not spiritual enough for the former materialist, certain as I am of the existence of a transcendent reality far more essential than material reality. Plato (I was passionate about Greek culture in my youth) speaks of Essences (that which is). A concept that commentators too often replace with that of Ideas (which are merely representations, therefore that which is not). The sublime world of Essences is thus transformed into a world of ideas. So much so that the unfortunate students no longer understand a single word of the emasculated pronouncements of the father of philosophers. The same incomprehension arises when he speaks of Uranian Eros, or celestial Eros, whose mission is supposedly to grant us access to the Essences.

Who, then, would dare to suggest a connection between the sex of men and the heaven of angels? It is this same kind of disconnect that thwarts attempts to interpret The *Garden of Earthly Delights*. The Neoplatonism that inspired the great painter was destined to suffer the same fate as traditional Platonism. These old discourses on Eros now seem unrealistic, fanciful, even infantile. If not perverse. In my opinion, this is because the relationship between sex and the sacred has been swept away by the disenchantment of the Enlightenment. We have lost sight of the mysteries of our own culture.

*Ultimately, it's our very conception of reality that is at stake*. It could be that our perception of reality isn't what it should be. The materialistic philosophies that permeate our thinking might not be the cause, but rather the consequence. The importance we place on material things—money, pleasures of all kinds—ultimately appears to be a symptom of a fundamental loss, all the more difficult to grasp because it is an omnipresent constant in our form of civilization.

We glimpse its echo in the myriad contradictions that permeate our history and our daily lives: religions preach love and promise us salvation, yet always divide and cause death, as evidenced by the Inquisition, the Crusades, or fundamentalism; the sciences promise us knowledge, then stumble upon the complexity of matter, the immeasurability of the universe, the endless inventiveness of Life, and remain incapable of halting the ecological disaster for which they are largely responsible; growth, the great goddess of our socio-economic structures, condemns us to an endless race and leads us inexorably to planetary collapse. Technological progress, meant to make our lives safer and more pleasant, culminates in chemical pollution and weapons of mass destruction...

Intuitively, we build our greatest youthful hopes on love. Every new couple tries it in turn. And what remains each time of those first flights of fancy? Almost invariably, forces of disappointment, reproach, rejection, if not despair, which we end up drowning in alcohol or some other remedy for oblivion. At best, in the boredom of a supposedly successful married life. With the alternative being the wounds of divorce, broken children, and the bitterness of shattered lives.

This contradiction is the first one against which The *Garden of Earthly Delights* could inoculate us. It is found there in the stark contrast between the second and third sections. The chiaroscuro could be dispelled, if only we could grasp the natural laws of Love with a capital L, whose innocent and luminous contours the painter sketches for us. The awareness he invites us to embrace could change our lives, transmute our relationship to Reality, to the Other, to Nature, to the Divine.

All things considered, Bosch's message, if understood and put into practice, could change the future of the world. It is true that if love functioned as we all aspire to see it function, everything would be different, perceived in a new light. The greatest wounds, the greatest traumas are directly linked to its failure, from the earliest childhood, with all the cascade of conflicts, dissatisfactions, and torments that mark our lives. This is where the endemic neurosis that is leading the world to its ruin takes root. But even more: if sacred Eros allowed us to rehabilitate extrasensory faculties, as Plato and Bosch suggest—and as my own experience has demonstrated—if they were part of our daily lives rather than being hijacked by a few scattered mediums or exploited by countless charlatans, our perception of reality would be transformed. Everything concerning our relationship with others and with the cosmos would be different. Choices would be guided by a higher power shared by all, events would find a meaning that the laws of chance cannot give them. Our lives would have a meaning far deeper than that which our intellectual projections attribute to them. *We are embarked, against our will, on a drunken ship*, a vessel of fools adrift without anyone knowing the course of hope, or understanding what is throwing our compasses into disarray.

Is this how nature wills it? Or is man the foolish architect of his own downfall? The question is fundamental, because in one case, all that remains is to shed tears to appease the Creator, and in the other, to actively root out the errors we commit. And a mistake, once recognized, is half forgiven... I am making the wager here, perhaps utopian, that I have discovered in Bosch the detailed and entirely coherent framework for a fundamental solution to the problem of our Judeo-Christian culture. Such a wager may seem mad, or at the very least recklessly ambitious. Yet it is the facts, the systematic observations marking the unusual path I have taken, shared by many close friends and acquaintances, that have convinced me of the necessity of this audacious undertaking. You will notice that I have deliberately not included a bibliography at the end of the book.

A list of references might give the impression of a thesis constructed entirely from established data. However, all the structured research conducted in the scientific tradition has yielded nothing regarding the interpretation of Bosch's symbolism. This is undoubtedly because he did not draw his symbolism from ordinary knowledge or contingent iconography, but from the much more subtle universe of what we might call "archetypes". A universe that, by its very nature, eludes the scientific approach because it transcends the laws of matter. We will return to this point later.

I have, however, supplemented my presentation with a brief autobiography. Many biographies have as their secret intention self-justification, self-refinement, the hope of leaving their mark on the century, a form of revenge against the boredom of daily life, or general incomprehension. These few pages of intimate and incongruous history cannot possibly fulfill these aims; quite the contrary. I offer these insights for the sake of objectivity and to facilitate access to the transcendent dimension of sexuality evoked in Bosch's work, somewhat against my will and at the expense of my image.

Hiding these obvious truths would weigh heavily on my conscience; I prefer to risk being misunderstood or vilified. At my age, I don't have much left to lose anyway. During the lectures I've given on Bosch over the past forty years, I've observed that the audience falls into two camps: those who felt they were experiencing a revelation, sometimes overly enthusiastic, and those who took me for a debauched visionary. The same division will likely emerge among my readers. If you belong to the first category, please feel free to send me any comments, confirmations, advice, or objections directly through my website. I will take them into account and try to respond personally. If you are among the opponents, please also send me your objections, protests, hatred, and outrage. 

One thing remains certain: the message conveyed by The *Garden of Earthly Delights* must be of paramount importance. Otherwise, this triptych would not have captivated the masses as it has for the past 500 years, nor prompted so many commentators to place it at the center of their attention. A recent historical discovery reinforces this view: in 1527, King Philip II of Spain, on his deathbed, requested to be allowed to contemplate the painting before departing this world. He is even said to have advised his children to study the immeasurable lessons of this work.
It is therefore our duty to do everything in our power to grasp its meaning.

Guy-Claude Burger
10 March 2017
