title: "Ecological hell"
weight: 17
---

A new phenomenon in human history, the loss of sacred Eros and the hegemony of the ego are having catastrophic consequences today in terms of mass consumption and population explosion.
Overconsumption is a consequence of greed, the need for compensation, and overinvestment in material possessions—behaviors that stem directly from the hypertrophy of the ego. The proliferation of the human species is also directly linked to sexuality, although these connections have not yet been fully elucidated by the scientific community. There are, of course, other factors, but the excess of the ego and sexual disorder are the two volcanoes fueling the destructiveness characteristic of our civilization.

The hypertrophy of the ego stems from the failure of love. Ultimately, it is the primary symptom of the erotic and spiritual bankruptcy of our rationalist culture. It not only has disastrous effects on the individual's spiritual development but, in a tragic boomerang effect of fate, also leads to the destruction of the civilization that gave rise to it, through the irreversible degradation of the natural environment. We thus arrive at this astonishing conclusion: the ecological disaster is directly linked to the errors of Judeo-Christian morality!

The forces underlying consumption are immeasurable. They are fueled on the surface by major economic interests, through advertising, lobbying, and multinational corporations. At a deeper level, it is the micro-dependencies of billions of consumers that form its fabric and give it its power. Without demand, there is no supply. The omnipotence of industry and finance would vanish if individuals preferred natural values and the beauty of original love to the false promises of purveyors of gadgets and illusions of all kinds.

The growing awareness of the ecological threat, fueled by the media in recent decades, is a major step forward. But will it be enough to quickly motivate the majority of the world's population? Will it translate into a general desire for austerity? Or, better yet, into a rediscovery of the benefits of responsible consumption, adapted to the true needs of human beings? We can question the effectiveness of the measures that different nations may or may not choose to take. Lobbies inevitably influence governments, and few large corporations would have any real interest in degrowth. When they adopt a green label, it is more often to boost their profits—competition demands it—than out of genuine ecological awareness. Politicians are poorly positioned to impose effective measures, as imposing restrictions or reforms would cost them too much in terms of votes. Paradoxically, liberal and democratic structures risk encouraging overconsumption and hastening disaster.

For example, carbon dioxide production: some time ago, with the Kyoto Protocol in mind, the predicted oil shortage reassured everyone worried about global warming. Then, the decision was made, without any qualms, to exploit the gigantic reserves of natural gas still available, then shale gas, and now offshore drilling sites, enough to produce CO2 at an unprecedented rate, much to the detriment of climate change deniers.

There is even reason to fear that the measures taken, while reassuring people, will in fact lead to increased consumption. Environmentalists speak of a "rebound effect". Making production less polluting can worsen pollution, because consumers no longer feel the same need to restrict their use. In 2008, France decided to reduce pesticide use by 50% over ten years. The result: sales increased by 9% in five years. The new ban on sales to individuals will change little in light of the masses of harmful products released by agrochemicals.

It is no exaggeration to say that consumption leads to a state of addiction. These are not merely technical dependencies, or habits formed in terms of comfort, convenience, and efficiency. The conditioning goes much further, to the point of putting minds in a state of shock, making objective reasoning impossible. A textbook example: the study of ice cores demonstrates that significant temperature increases have already occurred in the past, long before our industrial era. This is explained by variations in solar emissions. Consequently, many climate change deniers conclude that there is no cause for alarm or need for restrictive measures. However, basic logic dictates the opposite: that a natural increase in temperature necessitates an even more severe restriction of human-caused greenhouse gas emissions! It is clear that the two factors are compounded, making it all the more urgent to reduce the factor over which we have control. Yet, paradoxically, the continuation of human activities is justified under the pretext that they are not solely responsible…

Even worse: some malicious individuals are spreading the idea online that scientists are mistaken when they assert that CO2 produces a greenhouse effect. Whether this is a hoax stemming from human stupidity or the perversity of the oil industry, no one will ever know. The art of denying the obvious—in this case, the calculations and measurements of theoretical and experimental physics, the most indisputable of all sciences—can sway even the most innocent minds. Others claim that global warming is a pure fabrication, conveniently ignoring the melting of the polar ice caps and glaciers.

The dependence and blindness to which we are subjected are easily explained in terms of psychoanalysis. The Oedipus complex is universal in our cultural form. It results in the formation of ambivalent parental images: on the one hand, the loving mother and the protective father, and on the other, the castrating mother and the prohibitive father. We automatically project these positive images onto everything that is pleasant and reassuring, so that the infantile dependence we had on our parents is transferred to consumer goods and anything that flatters our ego. An unconscious confusion arises between the maternal breast, a symbol of love, and the food we find in restaurants, fast-food outlets, and supermarkets. Similarly, there is a confusion between the gifts we received as children and the objects enticing us with advertisements and shop windows. Furthermore, we identify with everything that aligns with the system (in the Name of the Father, as Lacan would say): media agendas, political promises, economic creeds, and traditional lifestyles.

All things considered, we are victims of a consumer neurosis. Neurosis is defined as the deviation of natural drives, with repressed instinctual energy fueling inappropriate or excessive impulses. This is precisely what is happening to us: the essential drives inherent in sacred Eros, unable to express themselves as nature intended, end up fueling drives that are more or less similar or substitutes. The natural aspiration toward spiritual evolution and a state of bliss degenerates into a need for immediate gratification, paving the way for the unbridled consumption of objects and information of all kinds.

The investment we should be making in transcendent values gives way to a systematic overvaluation of material values. We are, to some extent, convinced that happiness lies in comfort, in a mansion, in opulence. The illusion of being nothing but matter leads us to attach disproportionate importance to procreation. The subtle erotic impulses that the ancients attributed to the archetype of the sacred phallus are morphing into fantasies and practices that can have significant demographic repercussions, for example, by increasing the frequency of intercourse and accidental conceptions. And it is the natural environment that pays the price.

We are using the planet's resources at a rate at least five times greater than its potential for renewal, and this rate is only increasing. We pollute it more and more irreversibly each year. The brakes that international conventions attempt to impose, even assuming they result in real reductions in our ecological footprint, are negated by the rapid spread of the Third and Fourth Worlds.

Our consumption habits are spreading rapidly, affecting ever-increasing percentages of the population, and these populations are also growing rapidly. Given that all substitutes remain partially polluting, and that production reductions can have catastrophic consequences in terms of economic crisis, there is no guarantee that it will be possible to take measures to sufficiently reduce our current ecological footprint within a timeframe that avoids a point of no return.

In all likelihood, there will be no other solution than a radical shift in attitudes. If we want to work towards this, we must begin by understanding why our Western way of life is what it is, and why it is so contagious. This brings us directly back to the problem of the rise of materialism and its causes, foremost among which is the loss of sacred Eros: Bosch's message is therefore closely linked to the future of the planet and humanity.

It is not customary, in matters of ecology, to take sexual morality into account. Intimate life and the state of the planet may initially seem to us to be two separate domains. Yet, a deeper analysis reveals that Western morality lies at the root of ecological ills. The mechanisms are simple: prohibitions and guilt begin by causing the failure of sacred Eros. As Bosch's triptychs masterfully denounce, this blockage induces a whole series of disorders: feelings of guilt in the face of natural laws, intellectualization of romantic relationships out of a need for self-justification, frustration at the level of feelings of fulfillment following the disappearance of the magic of love, visceral aggression against others due to a need for scapegoats, depression induced by existential emptiness, sexual obsession, particularly with penetration, due to the descent into the biological, anxieties resulting from the failure of extrasensory faculties, and, consequently, a pathological need for compensation on each of these levels.

Where can we find easy compensation, if not in the capacity that matter might have to ensure our existential fulfillment? We are gradually replacing love with money, desire with gluttony, happiness with comfort, fulfillment with technological efficiency, the magic of love with romanticism or psychotropic drugs, transcendence with power or subjugation, the sacred phallus with sexual activism or pornography. On the scale of billions of individuals, the individual problem manifests itself in mass consumption and a deregulation of birth rates.

This situation is the starting point for a whole series of vicious cycles that feed on one another. The unconscious guilt associated with acting against natural laws compromises a state of innocence and unthinking. Intellectualization erodes the capacity to obey the subtle impulses of Eros, as well as the messages of the extrasensory realm. It stifles the magic of love and leads to inappropriate sexual relationships, which become traps due to orgasmic fixations. Frustration results in repetition and hasty achievements that only exacerbate deep-seated deficiencies. The search for compensation creates an inner conflict between the yearning for true love and the futility of material substitutes. The atrophy of extrasensory faculties induces existential anxieties, much like being lost in a desert, and we quickly take refuge in religions that promise eternal bliss, only to leave us unsatisfied once again.

Morality draws its strength from the fact that it promises to free us from this predicament: if you obey my principles, you will be accepted by both men and God. We place our trust in it as we once placed our trust in the love of our parents. It certainly protects us from all sorts of excessive behaviors and traumatic situations. But because it is unaware of the very existence of sacred Eros, it fires its weight in all directions and induces a series of perverse effects that thwart subtle drives. Access to the transcendent dimension does not develop as it should, leading to a frantic search for compensation. Since material or sexual compensations produce certain immediate gratifications, fixations quickly develop, fostering dependence.

This profound dissatisfaction also engenders aggressive or transgressive impulses, which necessitate a tightening of morality and law. Thus, the "system" inevitably establishes itself with ever-greater restrictions on individual freedoms and in a climate of underlying aggression. The need to defend oneself and assert one's deepest aspirations simultaneously feeds the ego. A return to greater freedom becomes impossible, because the drives inherent in sacred Eros cannot withstand the aggression or pressure of the ego. To break free from this vicious cycle, one must let go of the ego, calm aggressive impulses, abandon the various compensatory behaviors instilled through conditioning, and learn to be patient until love awakens.

Morality is instilled very early in childhood through discourse and education. A few contemptuous or accusatory words, a few images seen on television, a few guilt-ridden confidences can be enough to permanently damage one's conscience and set the vicious cycle in motion. Once begun, the process becomes self-perpetuating within the individual and spreads from one person to another: aggression begets aggression, compensatory behaviors create temptation, and egotistical behaviors elicit egotistical responses. It is therefore irreversible, addictive, and contagious. The result is widespread disenchantment and a headlong rush into consumerism.

Another mechanism further exacerbates the situation: the decline of extrasensory faculties profoundly alters humanity's relationship with its environment, depriving us of the warning signals that could make us aware of the dangers threatening us, particularly ecological ones. It also deprives us of the intuitions that would allow us to find solutions more respectful of nature. It is striking to hear some Native Americans speak of nature; they feel they communicate—not to say commune—with the trees, with the animals, with the elements, and are therefore inclined to respect the environment rather than exploit it.

We pride ourselves on having moved beyond animism, but by placing our sense of value in objects, in money, in human inventions, we have lost this "mystical participation" with the world as defined by Lévi-Brühl. Razing vast areas to build highways, exploiting mineral deposits to boost bank accounts, and clearing virgin forests to raise cattle seems normal, or at least necessary, to us, and we accept it without guilt. An Indigenous person, on the other hand, suffers to see a bulldozer disfigure a landscape, chainsaws raze a forest, or concrete seawalls encroach upon a beach. The last few centuries of materialism have deprived us of a sensitivity that might have prevented the anti-ecological evolution of which the planet is now a victim. At the root of this process lies, once again, the loss of sacred Eros and the extrasensory.

It all began with the power of the Church, which, under threat of hell, transformed natural innocence into allegiance to Judeo-Christian morality. Then, trust in the Church and its promises of paradise was reversed into faith in rational science and technological progress. Promises of paradise after death have given way to promises of paradise right now. The Marxist belief in happiness guaranteed by the sharing of wealth and industrial progress has contributed to glorifying materialism, with its corollaries of mechanization, wage labor, social injustices, revolutions, conflicts between nations, and pollution, even in the most remote corners of the planet. Consumerism, weapons of mass destruction, and ecological damage are now omnipresent.

Television and the internet further accelerate this contamination by creating a widespread "envy of the West", standardizing mindsets through the display of a seductive and harmful value system, combining the illusion of wealth, the illusion of coupledom, the illusion of power, and, of course, the illusion of pornography. Thus is created an unstoppable bundle of dark forces that guarantees the crushing of the subtle values of being and love. Where religious influence persists, fanaticism and weapons quickly emerge, generating in turn security measures that trap individuals in an increasingly repressive spiral, opening up an ever more universal field of action for repressive morality.

Nor should we neglect the influence of Western dietary habits. It is very difficult to measure the psychosocial effects of the widespread consumption of wheat, milk, and their derivatives. Wine, too, is gaining ground in regions where it had never flowed before. To what extent do the gluten in wheat, the exorphins in milk, caffeine, alcohol, and other nervous system stimulants influence psychological equilibrium, exacerbating certain impulses, or, conversely, inducing apathy and indifference to the damage inflicted on nature? The alterations may seem negligible at the individual level, but at the level of large numbers, even slight variations in averages are enough to cause serious and consequential problems.

For example, baboons that feed on table scraps modify their group behavior, becoming aggressive, thieving, neglecting their young, fighting more often and more violently, and mating indiscriminately. It is certain that aggressive and sexual drives of an animal nature are intensified by most stimulants. These are unfortunately drives that are detrimental to the fulfillment of sacred Eros, which, on the contrary, should be transcended. Was Bosch aware of the role of food? The cauldron on the nightjar's head and the spoons tucked into the boots of some of his characters seem to support this hypothesis.

From an ecological perspective, the Western diet requires large quantities of cereals, oils, beets or sugar cane, as well as pastures or cereals for livestock. The vast monocultures and deforestation, whose harmful effects we are now measuring in terms of erosion, CO2 emissions, depletion of groundwater, soil pollution, and, in the long term, the destruction of arable land, are themselves direct consequences of our eating habits. We are systematically destroying the humus, upon which our survival and that of future generations depends. If we ate like other primates—who consume neither grains nor dairy products, and very little meat—if we respected the same natural rules, the planetary problem would be largely solved.

The land currently cleared by monocultures would be covered with fruit orchards sheltering market gardens and natural meadows where poultry, small livestock, and bees would thrive. Vines, fruit-bearing vines, and berry bushes would hang from the branches or fill the gaps, increasing yields. The wood from the trees would sequester vast amounts of CO2, and could be used for construction or heating as a renewable energy source. The hydrological balance would be improved, and the wind would be slowed by the canopy. Erosion and land degradation, as well as methane, nitrate, and other pollutants from factory farms with thousands of pigs or thousands of cows, would be eliminated. No more battery hens in factory farms. Enormous biomasses would produce high-efficiency energy with a zero carbon footprint. The state of the planet would be radically transformed.

In addition to the agro-ecological consequences of our diet, there are also the altered individual behaviors resulting from the loss of sacred Eros. Our allegiance to materialism instills in billions of us an excessive interest in objects, gadgets, luxury items, opulent homes, high-end furniture, the latest appliances, the most up-to-date television, a more powerful car, a better camcorder, a more exotic vacation spot, and countless other compensations for daily boredom. The feeling of the futility of existence leads to the overvaluation of countless useless things. A profound awareness of spiritual failure drives us to seek material support for security, domination, or oblivion. The resulting phenomenal waste has major consequences for our ecological footprint.

The perceived importance of plastic packaging, for example, is linked to an exaggerated fear of aggression (damage, soiling, microbes), and therefore to anxieties related to the ego. The shine of plastic evokes festive packaging, and celebration is a substitute for spiritual fulfillment (see religious holidays). The pleasure of unwrapping mimics the pleasure of discovery, which would be a daily occurrence with normal access to archetypes, each glimpse constituting a new discovery. Then, health regulations and competition laws force companies to resort to increasingly sophisticated packaging, which now forms several "new continents", or vortexes of waste, in the oceans, poisoning marine life with nanoparticles.

The allure of consumer goods, the feeling that we need this or that gadget even though it is not essential, stems directly from the fact that they serve as a substitute for the loss of natural spiritual values. Concrete observation provides proof: the perceived importance of presentation, luxury, and material contingencies of any kind diminishes drastically as soon as we rediscover the natural functioning of Eros. Inner fulfillment means that we no longer have the same need to fill our stomachs, our eyes, our homes, or existence in general with material possessions. We simply discover that what is essential lies elsewhere.

One crucial point remains: population growth. With our Western lifestyles, we are already too numerous on Earth. Without wanting to delve into the endless debates of demographers, it is to be feared that a race against time will begin between, on the one hand, reducing the (currently largely hypothetical) average ecological footprint per individual, and on the other hand, increasing the human population. Now, it goes without saying that the reproduction of the species is directly related to the functioning of sexuality. This changes completely depending on whether sacred Eros is respected or not.

When the subtle values of love are at work, there are automatically fewer conceptions: On the one hand, there would be fewer accidental conceptions, given the predominance of polymorphous drives; on the other hand, there would be fewer inopportune, intentional conceptions, with "plugged-in" conceptions occurring only at the behest of transcendent forces. There would also be fewer surrogate babies, desired by parents as compensation for their sexual boredom or as a guarantee of strengthening the couple.

However, a minimal variation in the number of conceptions is enough to reverse a demographic curve. This curve is, in fact, in equilibrium around a given point, and a small increase in the birth rate causes the world's population to grow relatively quickly. The figures show that eliminating accidental conceptions would be enough to slow down, or even reverse, the curve of population growth. The conclusion is inescapable: the loss of sacred Eros is an undeniable factor in the population explosion, perhaps even its primary cause.

Currently, some nations are seeking to reverse their population growth. Others, on the contrary, welcome the population growth, notably France. This is a growth factor that delights all industrialists and the financial world. The situation could worsen the day ecological damage and the struggle for raw materials, starting with water, become a matter of survival. A nation's military power will then be crucial in securing essential resources, and each will have an interest in increasing its defense capabilities, its military personnel, and therefore its population. We can thus fear the slow emergence of a race for demographic expansion alongside an increasingly frenetic arms race. This should prompt a healthy pause regarding the famous commandment "be fruitful and multiply". This commandment appears in Genesis just after the Fall, and is therefore closely linked to the loss of sacred Eros.

A humanity truly driven by love for its neighbor, aware that essential values are spiritual ones, and in constant contact with archetypal truths, would be primarily concerned with the legacy it is about to bequeath to its children. Loving parents readily endure the sacrifices they impose upon themselves when it is for the good of their descendants. Currently, immediate interests, personal advantages, and the comforts of life matter on average far more than the happiness of future generations. The idea of bequeathing financial capital to one's children is also often a cause of irredeemable decline.

At this level of reasoning, we see that the failure of sacred Eros lies at the heart of the irreversible spiral of growth and planetary disaster that is engulfing our modern world. What about the reverse: would the re-establishment of this sacred Eros be enough to reverse the race toward catastrophe? Does the alternative Bosch depicts between the delights of his garden and the torments of his hell still represent a viable choice? Is the cognitive cost of questioning the system accessible to minds conditioned by centuries of materialism? Is there a threshold effect, a percentage of the population beyond which this new paradigm would spread spontaneously?

In such a complex field, speculation doesn't lead anywhere. The intricacies of the human soul and the diversity of situations make any prediction risky. For my part, I prefer to rely on observing the facts. And the people I've seen develop extrasensory abilities have all shown a much greater sensitivity to ecological issues. The importance of material possessions, money, comfort, and social status has systematically diminished, in most cases ultimately appearing as an illusion, in favor of a love for others and for life that has profoundly transformed their relationship to the world. Either because they developed a more subtle perception of reality, or because the extrasensory messages they began to receive prompted them to respect Nature, the primordial archetype if ever there was one.

Understanding Bosch's message, simply by restoring love to its rightful place, could change the course of history. It could first and foremost change each individual's life, profoundly, by restoring natural access to vital harmony, inner peace, and spiritual fulfillment. Through the individual, it could change society as a whole and the destiny of the planet. Reducing selfish demands, opening oneself to others, and embracing extrasensory guidance, both at the individual level and at the level of large groups, would sweep away the obstacles that thwart all intellectually constructed attempts at reform.

I conclude this work with the hope that I have contributed, however modestly, to making known and understood the mysteries of which Hieronymus Bosch was the heir. His message is truly redemptive, not in the sense of promising us a paradise beyond death, as religions do. He invites us to rediscover here below the earthly paradise, imbued with all its natural beauty, with which the Creator endowed us to guide our spiritual fulfillment. To finally experience this eternal Love, our daily bread, which it is entirely up to us to resurrect. Could the *Garden of Earthly Delights simply be the secret of the future...?
