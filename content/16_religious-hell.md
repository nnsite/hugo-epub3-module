Bosch has encrypted his message in a very hermetically sealed way, if we count the number of deciphering attempts that have failed in more than five centuries. Distinguished member of the Brotherhood of Notre Dame, close to the Free Spirit movement condemned in 1411, he was well placed to know that his works might come under the scrutiny of the Inquisitors. In 1486, when he was perhaps 36 years old, Le Malleus Maleficarum was published, breviary of the witch hunt, unleashing a wave of hysteria against any form of devilishness. Solid encryption was essential.

He had to believe deeply in the importance of the sacred Eros in order to sustain its lesson in such a context. He employed magnificently equivocation and stereotypes like a trap to in which potential accusers fell, shaped by the classic model of the triptych of the time: Fall, Sin, Hell.

Most modern exegetes still take their toll. We see those who succeed in finding, behind the images of perfect innocence of the central panel, connotations of carnal sin. It sufficed to skew the meaning of a few details to give a false impression of consistency. The prevalence of religious clichés was by far stronger at the end of the Middle Agesn explaining why no uninitiated contemporary has been able to pierce the mystery and recognize in the central panel a positive discourse on sex.

This reminds us of the classic drifts of prosecution investigations: anecdotal details, fortuitous coincidences, gratuitous analogies can lead the finest detectives to convince themselves of an innocent's guilt. Just as radiological images, produced by the superimposition of elements on different planes, can give the impression of a tumor where there is nothing, the false overlaps between unrelated clues sometimes corroborate totally erroneous certainties. Many miscarriages of justice are committed on the basis of presumptions of guilt, bringing together a cluster of evidence from random concomitances.

Bosch must be admired for his pictorial art and his inimitable symbolism, but also for his ability to deal with the weaknesses of the human mind in order to conceil his message. Too hermetic a coding would render his work useless. In order to bequeath something to posterity, he had to find a form of encryption giving a chance for open minds to understand the rebus. As we have seen, he relied on a very intuitive symbolism (in particular for those already familiar with archetypal symbolism), but which could also confound the inquisitors engulfed in their eschatological poncifs.

The keyword method is certainly the most effective way to elucidate a refractory symbol. For example, one of the most misinterpreted patterns in Hell is the "monster" in the centre of the panel. Many exegetes see in the giant egg a body, in the two vertical trunks of the legs, and consider that the head on the right belongs to this deformed creature. How can we explain the presence of perforating thorns, jugs and bagpipes?

On the other hand, starting from the most prominent keywords: perforation, pitcher, egg, hole, crotch, bagpipe, knowing that the message is likely to concern the relationship between man and woman staged in the foreground of Paradise, one quickly reaches the goal: giant gorge between two legs + perforating thorns = importance given to penetration; bagpipe = penile symbol + obsession (an instrument playing continuously); pitcher = female, therefore heterosexual penetration; vented egg = destruction of inner unity.

The other points belonging to the same motive must then, if it is on the right track, find a logical explanation: the face of the knight expresses both concupiscence and remorse; the kneeling maid pouring the wine to fill her jug refers to the tribulations the woman endures to make vaginal penetration work; her two horn cap identifies binary relations; the disc on the head and the demons that lead the faithful by turning around the bagpipe caricature the teaching given by the Church on sexuality and the obsessions resulting from frustration; the faithful on the ladder leading into the crevace, with on the head a chainmail and on the shoulder a sleeve from which hangs a pitcher symbolize the way to reach orgasm with vaginal penetration in mind; men tied up in front of a pitcher: the type of relationship between men that organizes around female obsession; the toad on which they sit: the fetid nature of their arrangement. A confirmation is still given by the pattern located at the bottom of one of the legs, showing a priest teaching his wife how to climb, etc.

This example perfectly illustrates the effectiveness of Bosch's encryption: spirits imbued with the Catholic creed seek a priori in the central panel the representation of lust, and in the right panel the condemnation of sin. So we need a sinner, whom we find in the heteroclite monster with egg body and repentant face. It takes an effigy of temptation, to which one reduces the course around the disc. And another punishment that one can see in the devouring by the devil. But the essential, the whole lesson about the sacred Eros, remains unperceptible, and the reading of the panel remains within the dominant norm without raising any suspicion. While a more neutral observer, or already little advanced on the path of initiation, wvill recognize an element (e.g. the crevace between the legs) from which to construct a relevant interpretation by connecting the dots.

One might wonder if perhaps Bosch's ability to find symbols so surprising and ever renewed, even if following some common denominators, came directly from the world of Archetypes. That he speaks of extrasensory faculties (the owl) could be the clue that he himself, was an authentic visionary. The countless visions that I had the opportunity to inventory among people in my entourage often involved displays of demons or infernal monsters, sometimes also luminous beings. Every facial expression, gestures, utensils charged with symbols such as funnels, crossbows, pitchers and others, ressembled those found in these triptychs. The most likely hypothesis is that such a fantasy production does not the result from a purely intellectual imagination, but of messages hailing directly from a higher dimension.

If so, it is better understood that the symbolism was able to fit just within the limits of legibility sufficient to keep at bay Inquisitors, while leaving a relatively easy access path to whom honestly sought initiation. The extrasensory provides much safer guarantee for this kind of adjustment than the best intellectual speculation.

It is difficult to know to what extent Bosch's message ended up getting across to the public. Certainly not with his exegetes, except Fraenger. Perhaps many unknown people over the centuries, have found in this triptych the keys to an initiation. They had good reasons to remain discreet. By nature, the Sacred Eros is silent in its experience and must remain confidential. Talking about it around you, boasting about it in one way or another, amounts to crushing it in the egg. Not to mention the risks of the stake until the 17th century, and of dicredit after that.

We might think that in modern times the progress of exegesis and its various logistical tools would facilitate decryption. Yet that would ignore the basal reductionism permeating our modern culture, and the antisexual moralism today narrower today than it was in the Middle Ages. Outside spaces policed by the Inquisition, there existed a great license which, although very far from an initiatory love, had the effect of freeing thought in matters of sexuality.

The Church herself condemned more specifically concupiscence, that is, the pursuit of pleasure in disobedience to the divine will --- or the precepts of the clergy. It mainly took into account penetration. It did not not initially stimatized what we would call polymorphic impulses, in which common sense did not really see as "sexual". Then over the centuries, the amalgams multiplied, turning all pleasure into a subject of shame and damnation.

For example, before the mid-18th century, masturbation did not attract the attention of educators or priests. It was doctors and moralists, not clergymen, who decreed, around 1750, that this abomination caused of a kyriella of diseases. The Church only required him to go to the confessional half a century later, to play catch up with medical progress, etching the concept of uncleanness into the minds definitively. Half a century ago, solitary vice was still a cause of deafness.

In the Middle Ages, it was common for three people to share a bed. Sexual hospitality still endured. The first room of the houses, where guests were welcomed, was the bedroom. Sexual intercourse was practiced in public baths, in front of everyone. This must be remembered, if we are to correctly frame the degree of encryption of these triptychs. Bosch had to protect himself from the clergymen of the Inquisition, but few of the ordinary people were those who would have offended themselves to see naked bodies mixed as seen in the central panel.

His message was actually more understandable in his time than it later became. It took the double tsunami of materialistic philosophies and bourgeois morality, stigmatizing everything that had a hint of transcendence or enjoyment, to make it totally hermetic. To understand his language, two conditions are required: not to be shocked by the naturist erotic of his earthly paradise, and at the same time not to give in to materialist reductionism. Even more: one must be prepared to admit the possibility of a sexual, extrasensory and spiritual function.

Are these conditions fulfilled today? One must mention Reich, who advocated for a return to natural morality. He described orgone, an energy both electrical, biological, orgastic and spiritual. Strangely, neither he nor any of his emulators mentioned Bosch's works.

There was also May 68 and his attempts at sexual liberation. We suddenly began to discuss masturbation, blowjob, sodomy in public places. Properly illustrated kamasutras were displayed in all windows. In the air floated the hope of a renewal both moral, social, anarchist, erotic, extrasensory, and spiritual: "Change your mind", "Make love, not war"... Along with sexual liberation, there existed a clear spiritual search: the deep impulses that led this movement, a youth-based movement, cumulated a protest against an authoritarian and castrative socio-educational system, and an enormous hope of finding the way back to more authentic human and spiritual values. The substance of these aspirations was undoubtedly a diffuse need to rehabilitate the sacred Eros. Alas, the healthy impulses were immediately parasitized by the use of drugs, a source of hallucinations conflated with a transcendent perception.

Any pendulum movement is followed by a return of the pendulum. Apart from a fairly broad recognition of homosexuality, in the name of the fight against discrimination, morals seem to have narrowed singularly in recent decades. The tantric anarchist ideal waned, overwhelmed by pornography, which justified a rise in moralism. "Normative" sexuality, that is, the equation "sex = reproduction = normal coitus", largely regained its place in popular thought and even among elites.

Of all these, there remains a cluster of various trends known as "New Age", seemingly resisting the tides o&f time. Everyone has heard about channeling. One can no longer condemn metapsychics as the Academy of Medicine in France did at the end of the 19th century. Already at the beginning of the 20th century, some scientists, especially in the United Kingdom, began researching the "paranormal". The notion of "parapsychology" began in 1934 in the USA with Rhine. From that moment on experimental protocols attempted to follow objective procedures and use statistics, more conclusive than rotating tables or ectoplasms. There was also the "Gnose of Princeton" in the 1960s, which attempted to create a place for the supernatural from the theory of quanta. Such a context should have facilitated the awareness of a transcendent reality in relation to life and love, thus decrypting Bosch's message.

The great difficulty of any research on extrasensory or psychokinesis lies in the rarity and unpredictability of phenomena. Science takes into account, by definition, only verifiable and reproducible phenomena. It even requires the underlying assumption reproducibility ought to be be verifiable within reasonable laboratory times. Let us admit a medium such as Uri Geller agreed to comply with an experimental protocol: if he can not bend the keys or spoons within the hours or days of the experiment, it is declared unsuccessful. Things would change considerably if the majority of the population had extrasensory visions or other faculties, at a rate sufficient for the phenomena to feature as part of everyday life, and with performances such as to restore confidence in the existence of a supramaterial dimension.

There is another obstacle: these phenomena require a particular state of consciousness. As Bosch tells us with his mock owl, non-thinking and concentration in the solar plexus (we should instead say "centering" not to confuse with intellectual concentration, the very opposite), are indispensable to the manifestations of the sacred Eros. These "modified states of consciousness", although recognized by most modern psychologists, appear unfortunately incompatible with the critical and analytical spirit indispensable to science. The door that the rationalist researcher tries to open to see the flickering flame of the extrasensory blows a cold wind that extinguishes it immediately.

Materialist reductionism is in itself a form of creed. It induces, in the face of a possible supernatural phenomenon, a whole network of thoughts, suspicions, contestations, fears. It acts as a kind of vaccine that destroys any embryo of extrasensory perception. It barricades the subtle areas of consciousness that should open to allow an ingress of metapsychic energy and nourish paranormal faculties.

We can even consider it, from the psychoanalytic point of view, an unconscious defense against subtle phenomena escaping our grasp and frightening us. Or that we feel guilty for losing. But a defence of this type inevitably generates a vicious circle: the tensions it induces due to its antinatural character, adding to the intellectualization of situations, only further derails the very subtle functions whose lack it reflects. Neo-positivism is probably one of the main cause, after morality, for the extrasensory deficiency that characterizes our form of culture.

The situation is no better on the side of supporters of the paranormal. They, too, at the time of providing evidence of the existence of a phenomenon, often out of anguish of failure, construct a maze of intentions, mistrust, justifications that disturb the very object of their search. To observe these phenomena would require both researchers indifferent to either their existence or inexistence and mediums insensitive to laboratory conditions, to the pressure of interrogations and to the rigidity of experimental protocols.

Religions themselves constitute a major obstacle to the resumption of the sacred Eros. This for three main reasons: they demonize the extrasensory, induce a prefabricated vision of the divinity and impose a counter-nature moral. The most common example being medieval Catholicism.

Between the 15th and 17th centuries, the Church engraved in the minds a dichotomy between the extrasensory phenomena that certain individuals could experience, immediately declared sorcerers or witches, and those that it attributed to Saints or saints. The latter were labelled as miracles, and the former deemed Satan's works, usually leading to the stake. The start of the Witch Hunt can be traced back to the bull of Pope Innocent VIII (1484). The Church eventually rose up against this practice which presupposed the presence of the Devil among men, while Christianity relegated him to Hell, but the kick-off had been given. As complex and debatable as the analysis of this period is, still today, in the common sense, a duplicity remains: a mistrust, a critical mind destructive regards to the "secular" paranormal phenomena and a tendency to adulation to miracles, such as those of Lourdes for example (even among those who deny them).

Second influence of religions: from the tenderest years of catechism, or from the first discourses of parents, themselves consciously or unconsciously inspired by religious beliefs, we are introduced to an image of the divinity that corresponds far remote from the reality of what can be called transcendence or the world of Archetypes. Like the animists who projected the image of sometimes benevolent or evil spirits on the forces of nature, we project an ambivalent paternal image on the divinity. The protector/interdictor father of our childhood finds himself floating on the clouds, and we lend him feelings and wills modelled on our psychic functioning, such as judgment, blessing, punishment. This form of iconography eventually obstructs extrasensory perception. Our biased representation of the divinity blocks our access to it, making us wait or fear manifestations that do not correspond to reality and come to inhibit our perceptual powers.

The subtle, though natural, states of consciousness are shaken, the blockade persists and sends the faithful back to his religious practices. Only the creed is left, which stimulates his intellect, faith, which arouses doubt, prayer, which exalts the image of God we imagine. The inner conflict ce between primary needs and contingent representations, then externalizes itself on various levels: contradiction between the desire for charity and the search for the material compensation of spiritual deficiency, tensions between the love of neighbor and egocentrism, disputes between religions and sects of all sides. Thus, a conflicting psychosocial firmly lodges itself, that makes access to transcendent perception even more unlikely.

The morals of most religions further complicate the situation. The rare opportunities to gather the subtle energies necessary to rebuild a semblance of access to transcendence from physical love face prohibitions and rigid principles. Every discordance between deep feelings and imposed ways triggers the mind. Failures result in feelings of frustration or guilt that further exclude subtle openness. Individual suffering also creates a need to impose the same taboos on all kinds of scapegoats, especially young people. The system grows its root in mores and those who dare to denounce it automatically become pariahs.

However, frustrations come with their share of consequences: they generate endemic neurosis, which can be found in underlying anxieties, aggressive tendencies, enmity, conjugal scenes, discrimination, racism, intercommunal hatred, nationalism, fascism, crimes, terrorism, wars in which our world is the scene. We believe ourselves freed from religions, at least from their worst drifts, while we flounder however we can in the miasms they have generated over the centuries.

The system is locked and confines our culture to permanent dissension. On the one hand, there are those who believe in the existence of extrasensory phenomena, and on the other hand, those who deny them just as earnestly. The score is about 50%. Then further aggravating the situation, superstitutions prey on the most gullible. A great many assign any event to supposed forces of destiny, without questioning the basis of their certainty. The fear of ghosts, revived by many television reports, still crosses many minds. Superstition offers zeteticians the perfect material to justify their positions. To them, anyone who does not deny the paranormal is superstitious, period. Reciprocal positions only worsen and exacerbate sterile tensions.

A more constructive attitude would be to wonder why, in our era marked by so-called objective science, superstition gains so many adherents. The answer derives immediately from the fact that extrasensory phenomena are too rare compared to what they should be if love were not sick. Everyone feels in his unconscious a frustration due to their absence, or even a visceral anxiety due to the loss of transcendent guidance. However, automatic defense against frustration or anxiety is either denial or reactionary formation. The first comes through in the form of overinvested skepticism, which zetics exemplifies by seeing only cheating everywhere. The second, by unconditional adherence to belief in any supernatural appearance, sweeping away elementary objectivity.

Much worse even, is the intellectual blockade to the very idea that Eros could be the way to an authentic spirituality. The rampant guilt induced by religions for millennia inevitably squats our unconscious. A few rare individuals have the chance to live, in their youth, the magical relationship between love and extrasensory. Most often, life takes them in a whirlwind leading them to hide their memories and minimize the importance of what they may have felt in exceptional moments. Social pressure, the obligation to do and to think like everyone else, the fear of losing (material) benefits, quickly dominate the most beautiful expectations. Material worries, the torments of love and the discourse on sex proceed to bury any non-conformist quest. In the end, everyone share the same rut.

Restoring a neutral, balanced position does not come easy, but it remains indispensable in order to rehabilitate the original values, remov ourselves from false certainty, and recognize significant phenomena. This evanescent balance, based on letting go and opening up to the unknown, is an essential condition for extrasensory faculties to develop as nature wants. As much as one loses on all tables by adhering to beliefs or refusing them a priori, one gains by fully distancing oneself from the need to believe or deny.

We ought to deepen this last notion in particular: the "need to believe". Where does this impulse come from in the face of an ideal, prompting us to adhere to it unreservedly, or even to hate those who do not, with as a corollary the need to convince? We could talk about proselyte impulse.

Blind faith and unconditional apostleship have spun the web of all religions and all ideals. Many are the chosen ones and the heralds who have become entangled in them like clumsy spiders caught in their own traps. Inquisition, Terror, totalitarianism, all fundamentalisms have their roots in them.

One may wonder if this need to believe and make believe is a primary need of human psyche. Is it a constitutional, innate, or, on the contrary, a secondary need, a false need induced by existential deficiencies inherent in an ill-embedded culture? Here again, the thesis of the sacred Eros, and the frustration associated with its failure, provides a decisive answer: if the human being is made to dispose, through the extrasensory perception, of permanent access to the transcendent dimension, it is understood that the atrophy of this function causes an unconscious panic, an incoercible panic like that of a pilot who suddenly would no longer have radar to go in the night. One hangs up by reflex to anything, which necessitates the banishment of any critical mind; To reassure one another, one strives to make others believe.

I have seen that people who developed their extrasensory faculties by living love according to the rules of this sacred Eros quickly find an inner balance both more stable and more tolerant. They freed themselves from all kinds of ideological addictions, stop pushing others to think like them, although remaining at the disposal of those who need help. The mere fact of finding love as nature wants generates the "let go" dear to esotericists, and restores a more authentic value scale. Metamorphosis is not done in a flash, no more than we teleport to the North Pole after finding a compass. The ego is always there looking, quick to get us back into the rets of illusion, justification, the will of power, but transmutation is at least possible.

The very fact that restoring the sacred Eros to function leads us to distance ourselves more easily from the ego refers us to another question: what is the origin of this ego that poisons our lives, makes us suffer when others have or are more than us, who tortures us when we are underestimated or insulted? If it serves something in terms of self-assertion, it becomes terribly cumbersome when it is oversized. It seems, in the current context, hypertrophied in almost all individuals. It appears as a more or less epidemic overinvestment of the self, which would not have to be, at least not at this point.

The thesis of the Sacred Eros also answers this question. The expression of love in society is only a parody of the original Eros, a counterfeit like those caricatured by the false cupidons of Bosch's Hell, armed with buttoned arches and beaked with spatula beaks. Human relations should be illuminated by permanent amorous magic, of which only a few remains in novels and films. Most teenagers feel its first fruits in their first love, but as they barely engage in more concrete relationships, inspired by the stereotypes of the environment, they see this flames smothered before finally considering it a dream of youth and a sign of immaturity. Almost all adults follow the same path, and this disenchanted world becomes the norm.

To the point that psychologists denounce as a form of regression living in a magical universe. The normal adult is the one who has exceeded this immaturity, who knows how to see reality for what it is. While the adult worthy of this name, which means "completed", should instead be the one who knows how to preserve the natural openness to magic.

Faced with the lack of this celestial manna, which is amorous magic, psychism reacts on different registers: by filling the void with an excessive self-love; by a pathological need for appropriation; by a need for domination and security; by a need to grow in order to better resist attacks; by hypermentalization, unknowingly aiming to uncover the causes of lack; or by a frantic sexual appetite, sometimes by irrepressible love passions. These are the main components of the ego.

On the other hand, the ego loses from its superb as soon as the real relationship with others finds place in everyday life. Love magic then acts as a balm of the soul, felt as a form of inner fullness that obviates the need to assert oneself by force, and heals the wounds of the past. Not that we weaken, but things return to their normal place and proportion. The need for omnipotence is empty of its substance.

For centuries we have also been trapped in a terrible vicious circle: the lack of love hypertrophy the ego, from childhood. Then the hypertrophied ego makes it impossible for us to access authentic love. Morality, which Bosch denounces as a cause of this failure, is ultimately only a secondary manifestation of the process. She draws her sources from the spontaneous feelings of guilt and anguish induced by the failure of love, the incoercibility of sexual impulses felt as dissonant, and the deprivation of this essential food of amorous magic. Guilt and anguish are precisely the main pillars of ego, a function of justification and security par excellence. There is therefore ipso facto a certain equivalence between morals and ego: both feed on the failure of the sacred Eros.

This equation may seem surprising. Yet, if we observe ourselves carefully, we see that we apply morals to be loved first by our parents, then by others in general, and to reassure ourselves of the criticisms or attacks that we may suffer. It is therefore a kind of tool of ego, whose aim is precisely to protect us and impose us. It would all go well if morality were in accordance with the natural laws of love: it would not produce any frustration or suffering. It would only guarantee for the good of all the preservation of the sacred Eros. And since it belongs to human nature, it would not be felt as a weight or deprivation. It would only be the bark protecting the fruit, and no fruit is embarrassed by its bark.

One can define an "original" ego and moral: what these psychic functions would be without the fallout of a culture too far removed from the original data of human nature. Part of the ego is indeed necessary to face many situations. Men living in primitive environment used to be confronted with all kinds of dangers, ferocious animals, poisonous snakes attacks by other tribes, droughts. Rivalries, jealousies, abuses certainly existed within the groups. It was necessary for a psychic instance to intervene forcefully to push individuals to defend themselves, fight against difficulties and save their skin.

The best model of reasoning consists in dividing the ego into two parts: a natural ego, charged with ensuring a legitimate self-preservation, to which is superimposed a pathological ego, resulting in unexpected alterations of psychic functioning (frustrations, anxieties, neurosis, paranoia). It is in fact this second part to which we allude whenever we speak of ego in a pejorative sense. The natural part is better known as "Me". Under natural conditions would remain only normal Self, able to estimate with mindfulness, objectivity and empathy dangerous situations and the answers they call for, but stripped of any destructiveness. It would be a useful tool in extreme situations, to be then stored away in a box after use.

Similarly for morals: one can oppose a natural moral, made to avoid errors and suffering, and a paradoxical moral, derived from cultural factors unsuitable for human psychism, inducing frustrations and painful internal conflicts. Today's morality feels like an effluence of the failure of the sacred Eros. Degraded sexuality, like any false enjoyment, is a ferment of attachment to unnatural situations, an addiction to degrading habits against which the individual must be protected.

Unfortunately, in ignorance of the lineaments of natural sexuality, the dominant moral gradually exceeded the appropriate bounds. It eventually prohibited love in situations where it should be essential to generate and transmit transcendent energies that could fuel our happiness and spiritual evolution. It has moved from a protective function to a severely counterproductive function, which destroys what it was supposed to safeguard. Then religions seized it to quench their power: the sexual prohibitions compromising access to transcendent faculties, they claimed a false monopoly of spirituality.

The antithesis, generally accepted, consider morals as a means of countering ego. It would lead the individual to tamper his own desires and wills and to comply with the demands of the group. It may have a federative function, bringing together members of the community on the basis of the same line of conduct and identity. This would hold true if this morality corresponded to the primary aspirations of the individual. But when it runs counter to vital impulses such as those of the Sacred Eros, whose stakes touch upon life or spiritual death, it induces on the opposite anxieties of death, reactions of aggression and self-defence that take precedence over deliberate efforts of harmony and submission. Instead of countering the ego, it feeds it daily.

Stoicians, then the Fathers of the Church or other pontiffs who were responsible for imposing a repressive morality on their subjects, accusing them and punishing them in case of disobediance, could only do so under the influence of their own ego. In the meanwhile the Sacred Eros had been forgotten for a long time (if not, they would have explained to their flocks what the Fall and Original Sin mean). They boasted their knowledge of good and evil are, arrogating the right to subject others to their own conceptions. They turned morals into an instrument of power, which could only strengthen the ego of their victims. The current morals are thus marked with the seal of ego down to its historical roots.

The most serious effect of ego is the hardening of the mind: the individual adheres to his opinions, justifies them, defends them against others and against his own doubts. Morality does the same: it causes a permanent conflict between the deep, irrepressible impulses, and the feelings of guilt it induces, hence a state of defense made of justification, superiority, which in turn cements the ego and hides sensitivities. Hardening prevents inner openness to subtle values, it extinguishes amorous magic, parasites extrasensory perception. The transcendent dimension of love is no longer accessible, frustration is established, sexuality is reduced to its partial impulses, whose compulsional pressure is in turn a source of guilt. Morality is both more necessary and more destructive.

This problem of civilization is admirably exposed in the "Tentation of Saint Antoine". Centuries ahead of his peers, Bosch denounces existing morality as a contradiction. It claims to ensure the Salvation of the soul, while also causing sexual obsession and spiritual deficiency. It only exacerbates concupiscence due to the inner void to which it leads. The problem does not lie solely in a libido stasis, in unfulfilled sexual impulses crying out in hunger as described in psychoanalysis. The want runs much deeper and can only be explained by a loss of transcendent energy.

To sum up: the *Garden of Earthly Delights* gives us the secrets of the sacred Eros and exposes us the consequences of its failure. *The Haywain* denounces the harmful effects of the family model and normative sexuality on society and on the individual. The Tentation of St.Antoine recounts the evils of religious morality and its prohibitions in matters of spiritual achievement.

At the intersection of the three triptychs is the stigmatization of a social and religious system that locks the individual into a vicious cycle without outcome and ends up crystallizing it on a mode of permanent conflict between natural aspirations and dominant stereotypes. The resulting tensions consume the failure of the heavenly Eros; the lack of energy is marked by a feeling of internal vacuum, of larval depression which underlies even the best conditions; Atrophy of the extrasensory faculties, absent in the dominant cultural framework, induces latent frustration and widespread existential anxiety.

The individual has no means to trace the causes of his ill-being, he has for any remedy only denial, self-justification, overinvestment of all kinds of objects or compensatory activities. Sometimes he clings to material substitutes, to objects, to money, to knowledge, to power, to art, sometimes to religious substitutes, prayer, faith, asceticism, chastity, promises of paradise, sometimes to all kinds of esoteric teachings or superstitions, but none of this brings him what he seeks deep down.

The sinister auspices of the infernal panels of these three triptychs are now more current than ever. Our society is built on this conflicting model and has recently contaminated the entire planet. Since internal conflicts always end up externalizing in the form of hatred, jealousy, aggression, prospects for the future appear rather bleak.

The background of Bosch's Hells seems a priori no more than unbridled fiction: ruins, fires, hordes of war, torture, hangings, drownings. A few centuries later, we discover a penetrating premonitory vision of our modern realities, prophecy of what remains of spirituality, and whose ramifications, for lack of premonition, display on illuminate our television screens.
