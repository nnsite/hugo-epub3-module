Originally, triptychs were intended to inspire devotion. They presided over prayers in some churches and especially in private chapels. They usually presented on the first panel the Fall, as described in Genesis; on the central panel, the sins in which humanity is crumbling; and on the right panel the punishments Hell reserves for sinners. The only way to escape the gemonies was Redemption, through faith in Christ and allegiance to the Holy Church. The reminding of Biblical values meant to bring the faithful back into the right path while instilling in them the fear of hell.
This traditional prescription is found in "*The Haywain Triptych*".
![](le_Chariot_de_Foin_HD.jpg "Le Chariot de Foin, Jheronimus Bosch (1501/1502), Musée du Prado, Madrid")
{.center}

![](chariot_foin_left.jpg)The left panel narrates the Fall in four perfectly rhythmic episodes: first, the fallen angels one sees falling clouds; below, Adam and Eve receiving the commandments of the Creator; in a median plane, the human-headed tenting snake that offers the apple to the original pair; and in the foreground, the angel Gabriel who expels the vile protagonists of Paradise.

![](Hieronymus_Bosch_-_Paradis.jpg)Nothing like this in the Paradise of the *Garden of Earthly Delights*: no fallen soul in heaven; a central pond that breathes purity and peace; Astonished Adam, a modest Eve, both radiant with youth and innocence, gathered together by a youthful Creator, with Christlike looks, looking straight into our eyes as if to take us to witness. No trace of a fall, temptation, expulsion, shame or guilt.

The two triptychs obviously do not deal with the same subject. Even though both of them depict Hell, one depicts the Fall while the other shows a relationship between man and potentially innocent woman, since the Creator oversees their union.

From the outset, we note that Bosch is very familiar with the Fall and the Original Sin when it is intended to do so. And the reciprocal: if we do not see in the central panel of the *Garden of Earthly Delights* nothing that evokes sin or shame, it is that Bosch wanted either to hide the sinful dimension of the scenes represented (as some exegetes want to believe), or to indicate the absence. The only way to decide is to examine which of the two hypotheses leads to the most coherent interpretation of the whole triptych, or even of all the works of the great Flemish painter who remained without satisfactory interpretation.
![](The_Hay_Wain_gauche_milieu.jpg)
{.center}

Just as we read a book from left to right, the left panels should be the presentation of the general theme, the introduction to the essay. The central panels can therefore be expected to deliver the development. In both triptychs, there is indeed continuity: the skies follow each other with exactly the same nuances; bushes that separate the background and the median plane draw the same movement and wear the same colour; At most one sees a difference of colour between the foreground of the left panel of the *Haywain* and that of the central panel, while the same soft green grass extends the paradise of the *Garden of Earthly Delights* and serves as the background for the whole central panel.

The basic logic would therefore like the central panels of these two triptychs to highlight for one the tribulations of the Fall; for the other, the achievements of the teaching that the eyes of the Creator augur well for.
![](chariot_foin_central.jpg)
{.center}

The hay cart, on which a family stands at first sight exemplary, a typical image of happiness in the home made of musical harmony and prayer to the Lord, is in fact paradoxically followed by characters increasingly tormented and demonic the nearer to the right side, with ever more violent and delirious scenes, a prelude to Hell on the other side.

![](lapin_surfant.jpg)The most plausible interpretation, at this early stage of the analysis, therefore seems to oppose that of most exegetes. It is generally accepted that the *Garden of Earthly Delights* is part of the great tradition of triptychs inspired by Catholic doctrine: the characters of the central panel would represent all forms of sin arising from the original fault. This thesis is also contradicted, as Fraenger notes, by the sudden rupture of colour and atmosphere between the central panel, luminous in its hues and joyful in its movements, and Hell where fire, darkness and images of suffering dominate.

In short: The Foin Char would confront us with the vicissitudes of earthly existence when it takes place under the aegis of original sin, while The *Garden of Earthly Delights* would offer us the spectacle of what love should be, if the divine laws had not been transgressed. Let us therefore put these two hypotheses and check in detail if they encounter no contradiction. It is on this condition that we can declare them valid. Let us begin with the Paradise of the *Garden of Earthly Delights* (we will deepen further the *Haywain Tryptich*). The eyes of the Creator who presides over the first meeting between Eve and Adam challenge us. First question: why a Christian figure evoking youth? Bosch certainly has an intention if he does not use the classic images of a God matured by an age portrayed by a white beard here.
{#jesus}

This one is not bearded, at most could we surmise him poorly shaved, unless it is a shadow effect. On the other hand, it wears superb light brown curls, somewhat seductive in appearance. Seen closely, we'd barely give him 20 years. His message could concern less the mature age, that of spiritual accomplishment or entanglement in sin, than the very onset of adulthood, with his great problem of the discovery of love, as emphasized by the teenage look of an Adam dazzled by the beauty of an innocent and modest Eve.

![](christ-petite-barbe.jpg)The most striking thing is its expression: the eyes wide open, the eyebrows slightly raised, the mouth somewhat left, as ready to speak. Yet it is not a Christian face, it lacks compassion or the mastery of internalized suffering. Here, the gaze calls upon the spectator, exhorts him to vigilance. Bosch adds to this call the signifying gesture of the right hand, both a symbol of wisdom and teaching, but also suggesting, through the inclined position of the fingers, a warning, an incentive to prudence. As he establish contact between between Eve, whom he gently guides on the wrist, and Adam, whose left foot twitches his own toes, the lesson seems to be summed up in these words: "Beware of transpires as man and woman approach one another, it requires wisdom and vigilance". Or: "The divinity must mediate this physical contact". The study of the triptych by X-ray or infra-red reveals a first face of the Creator staring at Adam. Change testifies to the significance of the lesson.

Fraenger sees in these three characters the celebration of the divine institution of marriage. Charles de Tolnay states that their apparent innocence actually hides the image of seduction, perversity and decadence. Faced with such differences, it is better to stick to what the painter certainly expected of the viewer: to start from scratch, forgetting everything else that was said after him and to let the pure message of the image, carried by the characters' expressions and gestures, resonate with natural intuition.

Two rabbits deliberately turn their backs on Eve. Both are smaller than a normal perspective would like. By analogy with the other rabbits of the triptych, this animal of as frequent as pelvic loves, symbolizes the coitus in what it can have mechanical (cf. the popular "hot rabbit"). ![](rabbit-symbolise-coitus.jpg)
If they ostensibly turn their backs on Eve, it is undoubtedly to emphasize that this aspect of sexuality must remain secondary, and that the principal is elsewhere.![](colonne-central-paradis.jpg)
Note that the rabbits, who look in the same direction as Adam, ride a kind of metal blade, which, as we shall see in Hell, can represent a more aggressive form of sexuality.

Another striking element: the parallelism between Adam's legs, and Eve's, which float lightly in the air, underline the gentle and regular slope of the slope ("acclivitas" wrote Fraenger, in the sense of "slope giving access to") at the top of which a tree stands.![](genese-idylique.jpg)
On this tree grow blackberries, some still red, others dark. As Fraenger noted, color metamorphosis can symbolize spiritual maturation. Red is the color of the body, blood, physical life, blue, often symbol of truth, is associated with spiritual fulfillment. Trilobed branches would be a reference to the Holy Trinity. Fine clusters of bright, crystal-clear flowers emerge discreetly above foliage, like so many revelations.

This is obviously the Tree of Knowledge with a great C, to which would give access, through a slow ascension, the loving relationship – lived in accordance with the laws of the Creator. God having created nature, one could translate into modern language (for atheists, rationalists, neo-positivists and reductionists of all sides): "in accordance with natural laws".

In Greek mythology, the owl was one of the attributes of Athena, goddess of wisdom, protector of the arts and sciences. Out of the head of Zeus, the master of Olympus, where all the gods lived, this goddess was half-sister of Hermes, messenger of the Gods to men. Renowned for her virginity, intuition and vision, she carried on her armament (her shield) the head of the Medusa, cut by Hercules (victory on the mind). ![](small-owl.jpg)Her pet bird clearly represented transcendent faculties. These characteristics are found in many cultures (Egyptians, Celtes, Welsh, Zoulous, Navajos...) In the Middle Ages, the owl remained the pet of the witches, knowing that witchcraft was not associated with the demon before the influence of Catholicism.

![](young_peacock.jpg)A young peacock, sacred bird in Hindu mythology and symbol of immortality, is perched on an efflorescence. We do not see here any ocelles, no doubt to suggest that spiritual maturation is necessary, in relation to the clairvoyance represented by the owl, located on the same level.

What can be the meaning of a similar aerial monument, whose base is an iris, and which points, like a bell tower arrow, towards the sky? If the owl represents the clairvoyance, it evokes a capacity or process that consecrates the message, as if it connected it to the sky. Bringing together crystal balls and pure water springs, it seems to reflect a relationship between energy and clairvoyance. The giraffe, the biped dog and the rabbit, on the right, an elephant surmounted by a monkey, a fox, on the left, all these eyes directed towards the central column indicate a phenomenon that surprises and exceeds the animal dimension.

Now, what function is able to consecrate a vision or intuition, to perpetuate it in memory, and to raise it to the rank of truth? A single function of transcendent essence can come into play, for whom it has lived: the one that Hinduists call "kundalini". Unsociable from an authentic vision, it is indeed it which comes naturally to seal the closest interpretation of truth, while creating a state of serenity and bliss. Like a superior, impenetrable guide, she is the one who knows how to lead, by bliss, the work of individuation described by Jung. Feeling in the form of a "sacred shiver", rising from the base of the spine to the top of the head, as if she were to open our eyes and bless the step towards the truth, she confirms every time we move on the right path of Knowledge.

It works for scientific advances as well as spiritual research. That is why we can consider it a kind of "truth compass" that provides nature. His loss exposes us to the wanderings of ignorance, to the hazards of arbitrariness, to the hegemony of ego. Our Western society is the sad scene. If our political leaders knew this guidance, they would distinguish good from bad decisions without straying into ways where people pay the ransom.

The widespread atrophy of kundalinî explains the discrediting of clairvoyance. Without it, visions and other extrasensory messages are difficult to interpret. It is quick to embark on false speculations, which one tries to impose on others in order to reassure himself, the forecasts do not come true, the mistrust settles, and it soon confuses extrasensory perception with charlatanism. The situation is even worse on a personal level: false interpretations lead to insecurity, doubt and self-justification. They ultimately contribute to strengthening the ego, just against the meaning of an authentic spiritual evolution. It is therefore understandable that Bosch places this fountain of life in the centre of paradise, in the axis of the iris and the Creator. The kundalinî is, with the extrasensory faculties it comes to crown, in the image of the arrow sitting on the base housing the owl, the central axis of true Knowledge.

Without knowing it, it is to this transcendent Knowledge that all our intellectual efforts tend, as the right-profile rock is magisterally portrayed. This universal aspiration, the very engine of science and culture, blind to the image of the eye obliterated by the mold (shell which we will see later the meaning), draws its strength from the unconscious and omnipresent need to recover the lost arcanes of the original Knowledge.

The kundalinî makes it possible to distinguish between the two forms of knowledge, just as it constitutes in the panel of Paradise a line of partition.
