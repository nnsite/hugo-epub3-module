title: "Crystal Balls"
weight: 22
---

On the evening of September 3rd, both my parents were out. I had gone to bed early, secretly intending to think of François in the privacy of my bed. That moment has remained vivid in my memory seventy years later. It will likely remain so until the end of my life. It was one of those vibrant late-summer evenings. Crickets chirped beneath the first stars. A dog barked in the distance; I perceived more of an echo, a sign of immensity, than an animal cry. Twilight cast a soft violet light through the large glass windows. I breathed deeply, trying to resurrect the magic of love from the past few weeks.

I was suddenly struck by the image of a large canal, opening before me, filled with a bright, crystalline, bluish light—turquoise, to be precise—where a few objects swirled: a kitchen stool, a woman's leg covered in a silk stocking held up by a garter. I have forgotten the rest. But suddenly I was pulled into this channel, then, after being carried by a slow vortex, I landed on the floor, in a setting whose every detail I recognized: François's room. He was standing about two meters away from me, his head bowed, looking down at the floor in front of him with a guilty expression, as he put on his pajamas. I could clearly see the tricolor stripes—white, beige, and reddish-brown—of the fabric, each perhaps three centimeters wide. But what was most striking was his downcast eyes.

After a few seconds, I told myself it wasn't possible. I had never seen such pajamas at his house; it must have been a dream. Yet I had remained completely conscious. Was it possible to see something from a distance? Immediately, the same moving channel carried me back to my bed. The vision had faded the very instant I began to think more actively again, to ask myself these questions.

Surprise kept me awake: could an image be transmitted over a distance, by unknown, supernatural means? I had heard of telepathy, but an image is more complicated than an idea or a state of mind. And why was François looking so confused, as if he didn't dare look me in the face, when he always loved to gaze into my eyes, so innocently? As if something had suddenly forbidden him to?

Then the thought came to me to think of my mother: not knowing exactly where my parents were, I felt a certain curiosity. Where could she be, and what could she be doing? Could another vision answer me? After a few moments of relaxation, the same bluish channel opened again; this time I was transported to the top of a grand staircase. A tall white door was ajar. Through the crack in the door, I could see my mother's head, with her round blue hat, looking back as if something threatened her.

I was struck by the oddly elongated shape of her head: distorted along an oblique line leaning forward, like when I projected an image with my magic lantern onto an inclined surface. This gave the scene an expression of great anxiety, of flight. Moreover, I had perfectly recognized the place: it was the third-floor landing of the Conservatory, where the institution's main hall was located. But what could my mother possibly be doing in that half-open door at that hour?

No sooner had I uttered this question than I was abruptly pulled back into bed. In any case, I needed a moment to think. The phenomenon could be due to chance, but managing to see a second scene remotely would have been a first step toward proving that remote viewing is a possible reality... Logically, I needed a third experience to confirm the others. This time, I began to think about my father, while trying to recapture the state of relaxation, inner peace, and simultaneously emptiness, that had preceded the other two instances.

It was more difficult. I struggled to forget my astonishment and my questions. It took a good ten minutes for something to happen. Suddenly, the same crystalline canal carried me this time to a kind of wall, in which a diaphragm opened, resembling on a very large scale the iris diaphragm of a camera. It wouldn't open completely; I could only see the part of the cello's tailpiece where the bow passes. I also glimpsed the bow stick, but something wasn't right: it jumped over the strings much too high, in a way that would make it impossible to produce a decent sound.

The diaphragm still resisted, and I had the sensation of turning around, whether intentionally or involuntarily, it was hard to say. This time, again in a rather limited field of vision, I saw the backside of someone sitting on a swivel chair, and that person completed a full rotation. At that moment, my vision widened, and I realized it was a conductor, judging by the distinctive baton waving in the air. I was then able to look in the other direction again: this time I saw my father in his entirety, sitting behind his cello, seemingly clowning around, his face jiggled, his bow bouncing across the strings as if mocking something.

The thought of having this third vision immediately brought me back to my pillow. It's hard not to think after such an experience. I decided that the next day I would question everyone involved to find out if what I had seen corresponded to reality, or was simply a figment of my imagination.

I was only thirteen years old, but already possessed a rather keen sense of objectivity. I'd had it even earlier: around the age of eight, I regularly pointed out to my parents that their reasoning didn't hold water, to the point that my mother nicknamed me "Contrarius". Perhaps it was this tendency, this need for logic, that later led me to study physics... So, here we are, the next day. 

At breakfast, I ask my mother what she was doing the previous evening at 9:30. She was at a recital for my father's students, as he couldn't attend. She was standing in for him with all the mothers who came to witness their offspring's performances. But the budding cellists make a lot of noise, and my mother had decided to leave right after the break, where she had been able to greet all the mothers. Her duty done, she slipped away quietly and made sure, as she went through the door, that no one was watching her.

The coincidence was striking. The time, the door just ajar, the elongated head representing worry, the fear of being watched—it was hard to attribute all of this to chance. My mother was very impressed by my newfound ability to see. She confessed that she believed in these phenomena: after running away from her family, she had seen her father, on the day of his death, approach her from behind, like a ghost, with the threatening gesture of someone about to grab someone by the shoulders. She had trembled for an hour.

Shortly after, I found myself in the schoolyard. The first day of the new school year, with a weight on my heart. I had chosen the Latin-Greek classes, for the sake of general knowledge. François, at his father's insistence, had had to enroll in Latin-English. I despaired of no longer being in the same class as him. His section was in another wing of the building. I found him by chance at the bottom of the stairs. He slipped me a small package, saying rather sadly, "It's for your birthday". I asked him what time he'd gone to bed. His father had sent him to bed at 9 o'clock, earlier than usual. That was the same time I'd seen him put on his pajamas.

I immediately asked him what color his pajamas were. He looked at me intently, with eyes I'd never seen on him before. I can still picture his exact expression. He stared at me, with eyes I'd never seen on him before. I can still picture his exact expression. Something both disapproving and helpless, almost contemptuous. I tried to explain the reason for my question. For the first time, he seemed suspicious of me. He probably didn't believe in visions, or perhaps something was still weighing on his mind. He finally answered me, though: his new pajamas did indeed have wide stripes, white, beige, and reddish-brown.

A third question burned on my tongue: why this awkwardness, this mistrust, so contrary to the routine of a year of open communication? He told me, letting himself be carried away by the flow of students, that he couldn't answer me. And that, in any case, we weren't in the same class anymore, that we wouldn't see each other again. I blurted out, "But you can always invite me to your place"... He didn't get a reply and disappeared into the crowd of unfamiliar faces in the enormous stairwell.

I remember the pain in my chest that followed. It was a return to school I hated, marked by disappointment, helplessness, and incomprehension. I had to wait three long years, until we both entered the Gymnasium (the Swiss name for high school), in advanced mathematics classes, to finally see each other again. We didn't hesitate for a moment to sit at the same desk, in the front row so we could follow the lessons properly. I was overjoyed by this unexpected reunion. It would, in fact, keep us together until the end of our university studies. Seven years during which we were able to prepare for all our exams together, share the same difficulties. We even returned to the property on Lake Murten and my parents' chalet.

But it was impossible to recapture the magic of our first honeymoon. The spell had been broken. The fairytale shattered. And I soon understood the whole story: that hangdog look in my thirteen-year-old self was unequivocally explained by a monumental scolding his self-righteous father had given him that very evening, threatening him with the worst of evils—homosexuality—because of his bad company, and ordering him to immediately straighten out the wrong path if he wanted to be even remotely worthy of his parents and his mother's aristocratic family.

François explained it to me with the utmost tact. He had no idea how relieved I was to understand the reason for this rupture that had so deeply affected me. Even today, I still feel a pang in my stomach when I dredge up those old memories. Sixty-seven years later, the wound is still raw. It's not a question of attachment, nor of disregard. Rather, it's the feeling of mourning, as if the destruction of such a love represented a life-altering catastrophe, far more serious than it appears. As if our culture doesn't provide us with the keys to truly understanding the importance, the function of these youthful impulses.

But I digress. That September 4th at noon, it was my birthday lunch. The most pressing matter was continuing my investigation with my father. What was he doing the previous evening between 9:45 and 10:00? He, too, was surprised by my question and pretended not to answer. Just before, I had secretly described my vision to my mother, as a witness. I eventually learned that the Lausanne Chamber Orchestra, of which my father was principal cellist, had been rehearsing an Offenbach operetta—a type of light music that the Thibaults and Casals of that ensemble considered beneath their instrumental talents—and that the evening had turned into a farcical affair. The conductor had indeed spun around in his swivel chair, and my father was even jumping his bow across his cello to join in the general mockery.

He was taken aback when my mother described my vision to him: nothing could have predicted such a situation, which practically never occurs in a serious orchestra like that. A conductor never spins around in his chair, nor does a cellist parody his art by jumping his bow. The ABCs of playing a stringed instrument are that the bow must remain firmly "on the string" if you want a pleasing sound and avoid those awful screeching noises. There's also "*spicato*", in which the bow jumps slightly, but that has nothing to do with chaotic jerking.

I myself was astonished. These three visions constituted irrefutable proof of the reality of clairvoyant phenomena. Such a series of coincidences is extremely improbable. I was therefore faced with a terrible dilemma: either the system of explaining the world that we were taught in school, which expressly excluded the supernatural was mistaken; or else these phenomena, which I had experienced firsthand, would have been nothing but chance or an illusion...

My mother was for the visions, my father against. Nothing would have changed his mind. And so, I was once again stirring up trouble in the long, tranquil river of my parents' marriage. My birthday meal turned into another domestic squabble. For once, my mother defended me. But it was more to safeguard her conviction that she had seen the ghost of her father, returned to torment her, than for the question of clairvoyance itself. I stayed just long enough to enjoy my slice of cake, closing my ears, like one of the three mystical monkeys that sat enthroned on the living room console, not far from the Buddha: knowing how to hear without listening, or knowing how to listen without hearing... Then I went to do something else, letting the controversy run its course.

François's gift, which I unwrapped first thing, opened a royal road for me. It was going to change my life. It was a book on electronics: "*La raidio, mais c'est très simple...*", by a certain Eugène Aisberg. An excellent, very instructive book, which can still be found today on online bookstores.

Partly out of grief, partly out of remembrance of that wonderful love, I imposed a strict discipline on myself: whenever I had a moment, I would sit on a chair in the hall, next to the radio, hoping to find some inspiration, and I would immerse myself in this book. 

It was a difficult subject, given the mathematical skills it required. All sorts of concepts that only came up later in the classical secondary school curriculum, where priority was given to the humanities.

I can still see myself, sitting between the hall table and our old Philips receiver, the book on my lap, setting off to conquer an unknown world, alone and without any help. François was no longer there to support me, and my parents didn't understand anything. But at the same time, I witnessed a strange phenomenon: as if an inexhaustible reservoir of inspiration had carried me, allowing me to grasp these mathematical and physical concepts more fluidly, eliminating all effort and giving way to a kind of awestruck wonder, even before the most daunting equations.

Psychologists would say I was projecting my feelings onto the gift that served as their substitute. Psychoanalysts would speak of sublimation. The word sublime would be well-chosen here. But it was about much more than projection or instinctual deviation, as Freud understood it. It was the very functioning of the most secret regions of the psyche that was at play: those where the creative inspiration Bergson speaks of is born. The will to learn here gives way to the magic of discovery, to spontaneous appropriation, to the sheer joy of understanding. Understanding then felt more like ecstasy than intellectual effort.

Within a month, I had absorbed all the fundamentals of mathematics and physics that would make these subjects, for the rest of my life, a source of joy, enchantment, and ever more rewarding and renewed discoveries. My grades climbed to the top, my term averages hovering between 9 and 10 until my baccalaureate exams. This was achieved without the slightest feeling of effort, duty, or work. From this love, brutally extinguished by the law of the father, I at least retained this legacy.

The intensity of these memories is taking me a little too far from my subject. Let's return to the time of my first visions. The uncertainty that my father's opposition to their authenticity instilled in me was about to be swept away by a new experience.

On the morning of April 8, 1948 (the date is forever etched in my memory), I was abruptly awakened by a terribly vivid image: the face of an old man, to whom my parents rented a wing of their mountain chalet, appeared to me in a bright light, on a white pillow, in a space also dazzlingly white. His forehead was furrowed, his cheeks flushed. His bloodshot eyes bulged outward in regular spasms, expressing great suffering. Then everything vanished.

I had barely recovered from the shock when the telephone rang in the hall. My father jumped up to answer the early morning call: it was the hospital in Vevey, the closest to our chalet, informing us that Mr. Galli had been admitted the previous evening, the victim of a stroke, and had just died. The hospital requested his family's address.

When I told my father I'd just had a vision of him dying, he simply refused to believe me. Hurt, I persisted, only to be accused of lying. From that day on, something was irrevocably broken in the trust a son should have in his father. Through this experience, the entire rationalist system of beliefs, certainties, and even scientific knowledge appeared to me as deceitful and fallaciously dominant in its reductionist tendencies. This shock would transform me into a budding, compulsive revolutionary, who would not rest until the entire dominant paradigm had been challenged to make way for another approach to reality.

The following summer, the school organized a holiday competition with an open theme. I decided to build a radio of my own design. In my parents' cabin, we only had direct current. The challenge, therefore, was to find a circuit that, despite this limitation, would offer above-average reception quality and sound power. I used all the knowledge I had acquired from my reading to design a suitable circuit myself, which I carefully recorded in my competition notebook. Then I had to build a chassis, install the Rimlock tube sockets (the cutting edge at the time), coils, resistors, capacitors, and other components that were still hard to find in stores.

I just managed to submit a perfectly functional device (except for some hissing on the longwave band) on time the following fall, which astonished the jury. I was sure I would win the competition with such a feat, so unusual for my age. I hadn't counted on a jury president who didn't exactly like me: in fact, the German teacher who had mocked my friendship with François two years earlier. He publicly declared that this competition entry was clearly the most impressive, but that he couldn't believe I had designed such an electronic circuit on my own at less than fourteen years old, that this made my work a mere technical achievement, and that, for this reason, my mark was lowered by two points. Nothing is worse than the feeling of injustice. I vowed never to submit another work to any competition whatsoever...

Fortunately, this disappointment did not diminish my love for mathematics. When, later, I read Plato, I realized that this was not a personal peculiarity. The great heir to Socratic wisdom speaks explicitly of two kinds of mathematics: ordinary mathematics, the kind taught to children, and true mathematics, which goes directly back to the Essences. It took me a long time to understand what this distinction could mean, even though I had personally experienced it and was reaping its benefits. These notions have been so thoroughly obscured by reductionist stereotypes in our culture. Crushed, too, by the school teaching of mathematics, which leaves little room for wonder.
