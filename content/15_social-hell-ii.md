Four hundred years before Reich, Bosch had already understood that the sexual system and marital confinement were the source of destructive impulses, lack of creativity, frustrations and obsessions that poison our culture and lead the spiritual evolution of the individual. For lack of access to the supersensitive world, and of proper functioning of the extrasensory faculties (the evanescent owl), the individual compensates by accumulating during his existence as many goods as possible (the char full of hay), and engages in the path of selfishness, possessiveness, conflict and spiritual failure. If Marx had known Bosch, he might have realized the deep origin of capitalism and consider reassessing his approach. Rather than attacking the injustice of the liberal system and advocate for revolutions whose outcomes we have witnessed, he would have denounced its roots.

But Bosch goes much further. He offers us a real psychoanalysis of Judeo-Christian society. *The Haywain* offers us the desperate and hopeless image of a society cut off from the original spiritual and sexual values, fallen into materialistic illusion, with all the concomitant jealousies, sufferings, ugliness that result from it. How could he discern in the 15th century the unconscious mechanisms that connect sexuality and the avatars of human psyche, while still today and despite Freud, few individuals can measure the importance of these unconscious mechanisms, it is a question that remains to be solved. Perhaps such insight was possible through the transcendent faculties, all of which suggest that he had developed them remarkably. The demons and symbols he delivers to us function as a direct reminder of the archetypal images received through extrasensory visions. I describe these ill-known phenomena in more detail in the second part of the book.

We still have to look at the foreground of the central panel. All commentators agree to make it a simple satire of the Church. But Bosch offers much more than a common philipic. He does not just reveal the paradoxes of a clergy unable to respect the rules which he claims to apply to the faithful. By considering the whole of this triptych, we can tease out the structural psychic processes that underlie a moral contrary to the natural laws of Eros and lead individuals, families and whole societies to the disenchantment characteristic of our Western civilization.

The Church advocates austere sexual morality, threatening with the worst punishment he or she who commits the sin of flesh, while the clergy deliver to it to whom better, while being paid to confess sinners. The sexual prohibition is so unnatural that its champions themselves fail to control themselves, as the Tentation of St.Antoine more explicitly exposes. We read between the lines that everything get back in order, could the heavenly Eros accomplish its divine function. It would defuse obsessions, and return to love its primary purpose. Do we not say "God is Love"? But not just any love would do, and we should rather not follow the example of the clergy.

Only destination left: Hell. The procession of the central panel leads us there almost seemlessly: the planes coincide, the feet of the walkers are at the same height, movements have the same rhythm, even demons are similar. The only difference is a naked man, in three stages of his initiatory journey. This side panel should therefore expose the consequences for the individual of the life mistakes decried in the central panel.

![](10000000000006A800001362879323C70840871D.jpg)In this Hell we find no image of the Devil, or any punishment worthy of purgatory or of a gehenna burning in Satan's fire. In the background, the silhouettes of a few ruins stand out on a fire background, placing the whole panel under the sign of destruction and curse. But the bulk of the surface bears at first sight the characteristic features of civilization. The dominant motif is the construction of a tower on which several workers work, equipped with masonry utensils and a long ladder. Everyone looks focused on his task. A high crane portends an impressive height for the building. On further inspection, the device turns out to be at the mercy of two demons. The rope pressing on the horizontal arm threatens to break and requires more effort to raise a load than would lifting it with bare hands. The tower is hollow and lets shine in the dark only the disturbing eyes of other demons. It rests on sewer, very ominous details regards to its stability and salubrity.
{.x-50}

As in the hell of the *Garden of Earthly Delights*, Bosch does not offer us a simple description of the sinner's tortures in the manner of the triptychs of the time. He gives us a detailed, psychoanalytic analysis of the mechanisms leading from the great stereotypes of the family couple and normal coitus to materialistic disenchantment and spiritual failure. This third panel details the avatars of our destinies marked by the desecration of the sacred Eros.

![](100000000000038600000432323CD266C3C30CBE.jpg)![](10000000000003BD000003516A2F073A099822CB.jpg)As if teleported from one panel to the other a damned arrives, driven by a deer looking straight into his eyes and by a demon with a wide open mouth. It wears on his lower abdomen a metal medallion (inhibition of subtle sexual functions), and on the head another medallion percied by a point (penetration and hardening). His false butterfly wings, at half mast, reminds us that false promises of spiritual evolution have marked the way, while the pearls that constellate its bliaut echo the repetitive sexual life to which morality leads. His screaming monkey mouth evokes greed, as did the hay of the central panel.

The damned turns to ask the young deer (what remains of his tenderness) what did not play in what he thought was his manhood. The position of his hands is that of a question about sex, and the deer points exactly at the location of the solar plexus with its leg. It was between these two areas of the body that the failure was articulated. The rear-train of the cervid emerges from the coat of a faun, a rural deity, whose too long tail reminds of the connotation sometimes divine or lubricated: the error consisted of confusing the sacred Eros and animality. The whole motif, in the context, eloquently testifies to the inability of the individual to find in normative sexuality, represented by the family on hay, the answer to its deep expectations.

Then the damned finds himself on the ground, he dies while a globose toad crawls on his pubis, blessed by another demon with false butterfly wings. The metasexual energy was desecrated to fuel a perverted sexuality, placed here under the auspices of the devil. The soul (the bird) can only see the collapse.

Two other masons apply themselves on the scaffolding. One stirs his mortar with a triangular trowel (the triangular constellation leads to hardening when we desecrate the sacred Eros). The one at the bottom, wearing red and the nose just as red, also fitted with a triangular trowel, is about to add a red brick (physical excitement), fascinated by the pitcher placed on the wall (excited by the fantasy of penetration). The load-lifter, whose pulleys are replaced by a horizontal beam, requires the excessive effort of the wheelman (sex deprived of its transcendent dimension only works with an exorbitant expenditure of vital energy). Behind the vault and windows we spot in the dark the eyes of an entire cohort of demons.

This unusual tower clearly represents the construction of the being or soul as it occurs during existence. Freud would talk about psychic structuring. The repetitive orgasms experienced in the wake of a desacralized Eros lead, as so many bricks, to build an empty consciousness of spiritual content, where the demons of envy, jealousy, depression, hatred, remorse and many others will sojourn.

The background depicts the world mercilessly as it takes shape on this erroneous basis. The glowing sky without a trace of blue promises nothing will rise above the physical plane. A building in ruins drawn over a fire (the form of culture built on top of this basis), a hanged person at the end of a beam held oblique by a demon as a sign of erection (disconnected sexuality, cause of spiritual death), dried trees (lack of transcendent energy) enumerates the marks of an existence without its supra-sensitive dimension, or even a materialistic civilization in which spread the vain constructions, painful hopes, unnecessary sufferings, illusory knowledge.

For those who would not have understood, Bosch is still detailing the message below, in front of a dark sewer from which fetid water flows. In his day, there was no talk of unconsciousness (the word dates back to the 19th century) and even less of impulse dynamics. Nevertheless, this foreground summarizes the underground mechanisms and energy disorders implemented by a sexuality violating the laws of the sacred Eros.

The most sophisticated explanation is given in the foreground by the "fusional relationship" between man and woman, deriding here the biblical formula "they shall become one flesh". The snake entangling itself between the woman's legs marks seduction and concupiscence. The giant fish, whose two antennas are dotted with pearls (binarity and repeatability), swallowing its prey, represents both the concupiscence and the bastardization of metasexual energy. A hibiscus barely out of the egg is wretchedly looking for something to pick on bare soil (lack of energy and failure of spiritual evolution).![](100000000000068D000002468499B877294FDF65.jpg)

There is indeed no fruit, neither red nor blue, in the whole triptych, unlike the *Garden of Earthly Delights*. This difference is not due to the temporal distance between the two works – they are very close and Bosch certainly did not suffer from amnesiac – but to the distance between their contents. There is no place in the materialistic universe of the *Haywain* for this notion of transcendent energy, omnipresent in the sacred Eros, whose loss comes across here as dry branches, empty tower, fire and darkness.

![](1000020100000224000000C6644C0FDE7E70907C.png)![](1000020100000518000003C3688839D4C57AC664.png)At the top, in the incendiary sky, a sinister echo of the desolation of the world plagued by the flames of passion and suffering, a dark form clings to its hallebarde, riding a faltered fish vanquished by a fuliginous flame, in pursuit of a black bird. The image is deciphered in contrast to the equivalent motif of the *Garden of Earthly Delights* where one sees a dolphin-knight riding a flying fish and being guided by the red spherule suspended from his cane. Within the framework of the Sacred Eros, the incarnation of the soul is guided by energy, even on the physical level; but when the fascination of the flesh has taken over, the fire of passion leads to an endless marathon forever precluding one to regain his soul.

![](100000ZREZFD.jpg)The message is further confirmed by the reverse of the external shutters. An old man, whom the exegetes have called by mutual agreement the "Traveller", or the "Packman", leaves, his eyes nostalgically turned towards the rear, a basket on his back, preparing to cross a stream on a dangerously lizarded board. Is he a simple pilgrim, defending himself against a wicked dog, embodying the insanity of human life exposed to attacks by robbers and punctuated with some folk dances as described in the background? Is this the mere result of a life coming to an end, lacking spiritual significance when it was dedicated to amassing material goods, as the hay stacked on the chariot of time will evoke?

![](100000000000048600000438218EB2C71FCDA0E8.jpg)As for the scene of robbers, it must be understood in the general sense of the triptych: it certainly does not denote the risk of a holdup, but a much more serious brigandage marking a past existence steered by the profaned Eros. Like the scenes of battle that take place at the foot of the hay chariot, the form of love represented by the family perched on its chariot, based on normal coitus and the absence of transcendent perception (the pitcher and owl) will have as a result attachment to material goods with all its avatars. And it is this attachment that constitutes the substance of banditry: covetousness, enrichment at the expense of others, social inequalities, competition, devouring taxation, and other economic characteristics that will mark history, and which still sign today the loss of essential values.

In the end, the Colporteur represents what man can take away from his earthly life as he is about to cross the threshold of the Beyond without having known the graces of the Sacred Eros. With only the memory of some material possessions in his basket on his back, a wooden spoon as gourmet souvenirs, his long dry, ithyphallic stick for relic of his loves, he gave for food to his soul (the black bird on the other side) only a few broken bones he abandoned at the edge of the road (residues of his actions without transcendent content).

A confirmation is still given by the famous medallion called "The Prodigal Son". A greyish character, perfect as the "Traveller", leaves behind him a house with a broken roof. Some see it as a whorehouse. The sin he just committed could account for the character's melancholy.

Looking at it more closely, and knowing what Bosch is trying to denounce, this rural home turns out to be a simple family home, all that is most exemplary at this time when the peasants were the majority. There you can see a couple, a girl or a maid watching through the window, laundry spread over a window edge, some farm animals, nothing that characterizes a brothel.

However, this image must be analysed in the line of the Chariot de Foin. There, the dominant motive was family. However, the family is based on the carnal bond between the husband and the wife: here too a couple is seen through the open door. So it is through it that we must start if we want to grasp Bosch's message.
