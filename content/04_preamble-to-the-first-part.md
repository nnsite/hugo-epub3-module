In Jérôme Bosch's century, the vocabulary was part of a Judeo-Christian reference, which the philosophies of the Enlightenment would radically reshape. Thus, in his pictorial discourse, we will find all sorts of concepts abandoned, aged today, or marked by a Catholicism that some might feel as outdated. To understand his message, however, it is essential to know how to decipher it by referring to the iconography of the time, even it means injecting modern concepts behind.

Some minds, in love with secularism or atheism, may experience discomfort, for example, in the face of words such as "creator", "devil", "sin", "spirituality", etc. However, we can not describe Bosch's work properly without employing these concepts. On the other hand, if we want to truly understand it, we *have* to know that behind each one lie essential and timeless realities.

The word "Creator" can, for atheists, cover perfectly acceptable notions in our rational culture, such as all the natural forces and laws that may have brought life out of inert matter. This should even suit creationists, since the Creator created natural laws. The same semantic problem arises for the concept of "Diable": it may cover all the destructive forces of the living, especially when they emanate from human beings with aggressive impulses or yield to desires that lead to situations of suffering.

The notion of "sin" can also be updated simply by returning to its Hebrew etymology: "sining" meant "missing its purpose". The sin of the flesh can thus characterize a way of living love that makes it miss its purpose, that is, contrary to natural laws. The concept of "Sacred Eros" itself refers not to something that would have been sacralized under the thumb of any particular religion, but to the subtle form of love that allows to develop extrasensory perception and reach the world of Archetypes, like tantrism claims to. The "Fall" is then no other than the loss of this transcendent dimension of love, taking place *both* in the protohistory of humanity and in the course of every human existence.

The word "magic" today evokes more prestidigitation than any transcendence. "Amorous magic", this feeling of elevation and vibration that occurs at the beginning of a relationship, is reduced, under the aegis of romanticism or neuropsychology, to a purely emotional phenomenon or even a discharge of dopamine. Knowing its close relationship with the development of extrasensory faculties, we must bring back to it the dimension that Novalis evoked, for example, by speaking of "elective affinities" and which all poets of love sing. His "sacred" dimension must be restored.

The spirituality involved in Bosch is not what is generally understood, in our time as well as in his, and which presupposes allegiance to a predefined religion or creed. *Here we are talking about the metamorphoses of consciousness* operating through messages accessible by way of extrasensory perception, what C.G. Jung calls the "*individuation process*". The "solar plexus", now medicalized in the form of peri-duodenal ganglia, represents here the center of perception of the "numinous energies" associated with archetypes.

Be wary of the risk of misunderstandings when it comes to extrasensory perception. For modern man, paranormal faculties evoke more deception or superstition than to any spiritual dimension. This is the result of an significant transformation that has occurred under the pressure of both the Church and rationalism. The first wanted to appropriate "miracles" by attributing them to a list of approved Saints---or to Satan when it couldn't---while the second simply denied anything escaping materialistic explanations. *In Bosch, the original unity between extrasensory and spiritual*, between magic and sacred, between divination and divinity *is immanent*.

The great originality of Bosch's teaching is to connect the spiritual to the carnal, the love of God to physical love, the later ensuring the acquisition of the subtle energies necessary for the natural development of supra-sensory faculties, allowing then these faculties to play a key role in spiritual evolution. A bridge with two arches is thus thrown between the biological and the spiritual, rendering moot the conflicts and contradictions that arise when one claims to separate the body and the spirit, including the apparent incompatibilities between religious conceptions and rational conceptions.

These reservations being made, each of the many symbols used by Bosch finds an explanation perfectly integrating a broader significance. I could be criticized for overanalyzing details of a work where the unbridled imagination should leave more freedom to fantasy. This position, however, opens the way for all kinds of off-kilter interpretations. *It is better to start from the point of view that the great painter has very spontaneously inscribed every detail* of his work in the general line of a message he intended to convey. Painting a face, a demon, an animal did not occur by pressing a computer button. The material realization of each detail, and previously the work necessary for its conception, allowed time to organize and lay it out in a coherent way.

We therefore embark on an exegesis meant to be exhaustive and self-consistent.
